# Chapter 4: The Engine Room — FII/DII Flows

Every daily market commentary in this corpus begins the same way: "FIIs sold ₹X crore, DIIs bought ₹Y crore." The numbers change; the structure does not. Foreign Institutional Investors (FIIs) and Domestic Institutional Investors (DIIs) — primarily mutual funds and insurance companies — are the two giant pumps that drive the liquidity engine of Indian equity markets. Understanding who is buying, who is selling, and how the gap between the two resolves is the most basic skill of reading the Indian market.

This chapter extracts the patterns across eight years of data — from October 2018, when FIIs sold a record ₹30,000 crore in a single month, to August 2026, when they crossed cumulative ₹8 lakh crore of net selling since 2022. The numbers are staggering. The lessons are subtler than the headlines suggest.


## 1. The Stubborn Significance of FII Flows

In classic market theory, foreign flows are a leading indicator. When FIIs sell persistently, the market is expected to fall. When they buy, the market rises. For much of the 2000s and 2010s, this correlation held: a US$1 billion FII outflow reliably knocked the Sensex down by a measurable percentage.

But beginning around 2020, the correlation weakened dramatically. Between October 2021 and December 2024, FIIs sold more than US$40 billion worth of Indian equities — an amount that would, under the old correlation, have driven the market down by tens of thousands of points. The actual outcome? The Nifty rose from roughly 17,500 in October 2021 to over 26,000 by the end of 2024.

What changed? The domestic institutional base. India's mutual fund industry grew its equity assets under management from roughly ₹10 lakh crore in 2020 to over ₹35 lakh crore by 2025. Monthly SIP flows crossed ₹20,000 crore. Insurance companies added another ₹10,000-15,000 crore per quarter. **A domestic buying engine emerged that could absorb almost any level of FII selling.**

In the four trading sessions ending August 8, 2024 — during the global yen-carry crash — FIIs sold ₹20,000 crore. DIIs bought ₹20,000 crore. The market did not collapse. The baton had passed.

> *"Once upon a time I remember if FIIs sell US$1 billion, Nifty would fall 3-4%. US$200 billion would have meant a 10% fall. Now that is not happening. Why? Because domestic institutions are willing to absorb no matter how much the FIIs sell."*

This is not to say FII flows are irrelevant. They remain the marginal price-setter on most days. But their significance is no longer proportional to the headline number. A day when FIIs sell ₹8,000 crore and DIIs buy ₹3,500 crore is a net-selling day of ₹4,500 crore — still heavy, but survivable. The old expectation of a crash from that level no longer holds.


## 2. Reading the Direction, Not Just the Number

The headline figure — "FIIs sold ₹5,000 crore today" — is the least useful part of the data. What matters is **how** they sold, and in what instrument.

**FII cash-market activity** — buying and selling of listed equities — is what the evening news reports. But FIIs also participate through the F&O segment, and their positioning in index futures is often a better directional signal than cash flows.

A pattern observed repeatedly in this corpus: when FIIs are net sellers in cash but maintain a large long position in index futures, the selling is likely temporary — hedging, not conviction. When FII futures longs fall below 50% of their total open interest while cash selling continues, the selling is structural — they are reducing exposure to India as an asset class.

The second distinction is between a **single large block deal** and a **diffuse sell-off**. A block trade of ₹6,800 crore — such as the Kotak Mahindra Bank block in March 2022, where a Canadian pension fund exited its entire position — represents a single institutional decision, not a systemic capital outflow. The market absorbs it within days. A sustained ₹2,000-3,000 crore per day of across-the-board selling for four consecutive weeks is a different signal entirely.

> *"A block deal is one seller and one buyer. It's not FIIs exiting India — it's one fund rebalancing. The daily cash flow data lumps them together."*


## 3. The FII-Crunch Triggers

Across the eight years covered in this corpus, FII selling episodes cluster around four identifiable triggers:

**Tax policy changes.** The 2023 budget's removal of indexation benefits for debt funds and the 2024 increase in long-term capital gains tax both triggered multi-week FII outflows. The Mauritius tax treaty change in early 2024 caused a particularly sharp ₹50,000 crore outflow over six weeks. FIIs allocate capital based on post-tax returns, and a tax change that retroactively affects existing investments is treated as a breach of trust — capital is repatriated first, questions are asked later.

**Election uncertainty.** The pattern is remarkably consistent across 2014, 2019, and 2024: in the six months before a general election, FIIs lighten their India exposure. If the outcome is a clear majority, they return within weeks. If the outcome is a coalition, the return is slower and conditional on policy stability. In 2024, the exit polls predicted a landslide for the incumbent; when the actual result was a slimmer majority, FIIs accelerated their selling, and the market crashed 9% intraday on June 4 before recovering.

**Global rate differentials.** When US interest rates rise, the carry trade advantage of holding Indian equities shrinks. The 2022 Fed hiking cycle — 500 basis points in twelve months — triggered a 15-month FII selling streak. The flow only stabilised when US rates peaked and the expectation of cuts began to build. This is not a reflection on India's fundamentals; it is a mechanical portfolio reallocation by global asset allocators.

**Valuation perception.** At various points — early 2022, late 2024 — FIIs communicated through their flows that they considered Indian equity valuations stretched relative to other emerging markets. The data support this: when the Nifty P/E crosses 23-24, FIIs tend to reduce exposure. When valuations compress back to 19-20, they return. The correlation is not perfect, but it is persistent enough to use as a rough timing filter.


## 4. DIIs — The Stabilising Force

Domestic institutional investors (mutual funds, insurance, pension funds) now manage assets so large that their monthly SIP inflows alone — roughly ₹25,000 crore by 2025-26 — are larger than most months' FII net flows. This structural monthly buying acts as a floor under the market.

The mechanism is simple: a mutual fund that receives ₹1,000 crore in SIP inflows on the first of every month must invest that money within a few weeks, regardless of the market level. When the market is falling, this buying provides support. When the market is rising, it adds momentum. The net effect is that **Indian markets are now structurally bid** on any significant dip, because the DII buying queue is always present.

This is not bullish in the sense that markets always rise. It means that the drawdown profile of Indian markets has changed: the sharp, panic-driven crashes of 2008 and 2020 are less likely to repeat in exactly the same form because the DII absorption mechanism is 5-10 times larger than it was during those events. The 2024 election-day crash of 9% was reversed within a week — partly because DIIs bought nearly ₹15,000 crore on the day of the crash itself.

> *"No matter how much the FIIs are selling, the domestic institutions are willing to absorb. If FIIs sold ₹20,000 crore in the last four trading sessions, the DIIs bought the same ₹20,000 crore. That is why markets did not fall more."*


## 5. ADR Read-Throughs and Global Bearing

A specialised but useful technique documented extensively in this corpus is the **ADR read-through**. Many large Indian companies — Infosys, ICICI Bank, HDFC Bank, Tata Motors — also trade on US exchanges as American Depositary Receipts (ADRs). The US market closes after India, so the ADR's overnight price movement provides a real-time signal of how global investors are re-rating the company after its domestic close.

On earnings days, the ADR is particularly valuable. A company reports results during Indian trading hours; the stock reacts; US-based investors, who hold the ADR, absorb the information and trade it during the US session. The next morning, the Indian stock often gaps to the ADR's post-close level. A trader who watches the ADR can anticipate the gap.

> *"If TCS or Infosys results come out today, I will watch how the ADR trades tonight. If the ADR is up 3% in US trading, our market will gap up by at least that much tomorrow."*

The read-through is not mechanical. Currency fluctuations, US sector sentiment on the same day, and corporate actions can distort the relationship. But as a general directional indicator for the next open, the ADR is more reliable than most pre-market commentary.


## 6. When the Baton Passes

The long-term structural shift is clear: **India is becoming a domestically-driven market.** The proportion of FII ownership in Indian equities has declined from a peak of roughly 25% in 2010 to under 18% by 2025, while domestic institutional ownership has correspondingly risen. This does not mean FIIs are irrelevant — they still trade with larger ticket sizes and faster reaction times than most DIIs. But it does mean that market crashes driven by FII selling alone are increasingly self-limiting: the DII bid absorbs the supply, the market stabilises, and the narrative shifts.

The implication for the trader is that **old correlations should not be trusted without re-benchmarking.** A strategy built on the assumption that ₹10,000 crore of net FII selling predicts a 3% fall will be wrong in 2025-26, because the domestic bid has changed the market structure. The trader must monitor not just the flow number, but the absorption capacity that determines its impact.

---

### Key Takeaways

1. FII selling is less impactful than in the past because DII absorption has grown 5-10×. The headline number matters less than who is on the other side of the trade.

2. Look beyond the cash flow number. FII positioning in index futures and the nature of the selling (block vs diffuse) provide better directional signals.

3. FII selling clusters around four triggers: tax changes, election uncertainty, global rate differentials, and valuation perception. Each implies a different holding period for the flow.

4. DII flows are structurally stabilizing: monthly SIP and insurance inflows create a permanent bid that markets into dips.

5. ADR read-throughs provide a real-time signal of US-based re-pricing for dual-listed Indian stocks, particularly around earnings.

6. The domestic bid has changed the market structure. Strategies built on pre-2020 FII correlations need recalibration.
