# Chapter 6: Reading the Option Chain

The option chain — the grid of strike prices with their corresponding bid-ask premiums, open interest, and implied volatilities — is the Indian market's most underused source of real-time intelligence. Every trading day, between 10:30 AM and the close, the option chain tells you where the institutional crowd thinks the market can go, where they are positioned, and at what level they will fight to defend their positions. The skill is in knowing how to read it.

This chapter covers the three most reliable signals from the option chain that appear repeatedly across the eight years of daily market commentary: max-pain open interest, breakeven-derived support and resistance, and the positioning asymmetry signal.


## 1. Max-Pain Open Interest

The simplest observation: at any given time, the option chain has a strike price with the highest concentration of open interest on both the call and put sides. This level — the **max-pain strike** — is the price at which the maximum number of options (by open interest) would expire worthless, causing maximum financial pain to the maximum number of option buyers.

Market makers are aware of this. The professional community that writes options knows where the pain is concentrated and tends to defend that level in the final days before expiry. The result is that the market often gravitates toward the max-pain level in the last week of a series — not by conspiracy, but by the mechanical reality that the largest option-writing positions are hedged around these strikes, and the hedging flows themselves push the market toward the strike the writers need.

> *"If there is maximum open interest at 17,500 call and 17,500 put, the market is likely to close around 17,500 on expiry day. The writers will defend that level."*

**The caveat:** max-pain works best in the final 5-7 days of an expiry series. At the beginning of a series, open interest is still being accumulated and the max-pain signal is noisy. By the second half of the series, the hedging flows become self-reinforcing, and the max-pain level can be read with greater confidence.

**The second-order signal:** when the max-pain level shifts from one strike to another — for example, when the 17,500 call's open interest declines and the 18,000 call's OI builds — it signals that the writing community has accepted a higher range. This is a bullish signal. Similarly, a downward shift in max-pain strikes is bearish. The market is not predicting direction; the option writers are adjusting to a new expected range, and their hedging flows will reinforce the new level.


## 2. Breakeven Support and Resistance

The option chain provides not just where positions are concentrated but where the market's participants *can* survive. The breakeven of a short option position is the strike price plus or minus the premium collected. If a trader sells a 17,500 put for ₹100, their breakeven is 17,400. The market can fall 100 points and they still break even. Below 17,400, they start losing money — and they have an incentive to hedge, exit, or defend.

This makes the **breakeven of the highest-open-interest strikes** the true support and resistance levels of the market — not the chart patterns.

> *"Max OI put strike 17,700 minus premium = breakeven support 17,650. That is where the sellers' cushion ends. Below that, they start defending."*

A worked example from a 2023 daily commentary: the highest-OI put was at 17,700, trading at a premium of ₹50. The breakeven was 17,650. The highest-OI call was at 18,000, trading at ₹45. The upside breakeven was 18,045. The market's expected range was 17,650-18,045 — a 395-point channel. As long as Nifty stayed within this range, the short-option writers would keep their premium, and their hedging flows would tend to push the market back into the range if it threatened to break out.

A trader using this framework does not need to guess where the market is going. They can position for range-bound behaviour — selling the wings of the expected range and collecting premium — and adjust only when the range itself shifts.


## 3. The Positioning Asymmetry Signal

The max-pain and breakeven frameworks work in normal conditions. But some days the market is positioned asymmetrically — one side has far more open interest than the other. In those conditions, the option chain tells you not just the range but the *direction of risk.*

Consider a series where there are five times as many call writers as put writers. The market rallies. The call writers, who are losing money as Nifty approaches their strikes, must either cover their short calls (by buying them back) or roll them to higher strikes. Both actions are themselves bullish — the covering puts more upward pressure on the call premiums, and the rolling accepts that the market has moved higher. This is why heavily one-sided positioning tends to resolve in the direction of the asymmetry.

> *"If everyone has written calls and nobody has written puts, the upside surprise risk dominates. The expiry resolves upward."*

The corollary is equally important: when the positioning asymmetry is extreme and the market has already moved in the direction of the crowded side, the fuel for a continuation is limited. The large majority of call writers who were going to be squeezed have already been squeezed. The remaining position is held by the most stubborn participants — and the market, sensing no further resistance, may reverse.

This is where the option chain becomes a contrarian indicator. **High open interest at a strike that is about to be reached is a brake on the move. Low open interest at a strike beyond is a vacuum that the market can fill quickly.**


## 4. PCR Done Right

The Put-Call Ratio (PCR) — the ratio of put open interest to call open interest — is one of the most commonly cited but most commonly misread indicators in Indian market commentary.

A PCR above 1.2 is usually considered bearish (puts are crowded, so everyone expects a fall, which means the market may rise). A PCR below 0.8 is considered bullish (calls are crowded, so a fall may come). This is a rough heuristic that works in ranges but fails in extremes.

A more reliable signal comes from the **PCR at specific strikes** rather than across the entire chain. If the PCR at strikes 2-3% below the current market is very high (meaning puts are concentrated at levels that are already out of the money), it signals that the market's put writers have built their defence below where the market is trading. This is a baseline of support. If the same measure at strikes 2-3% above is low, it signals that call resistance is weak — a vacuum that an unexpected rally can fill.

> *"Looking at open interest across the whole chain tells you where the crowd is. Looking at the PCR of strikes within a range of the current price tells you where the real defence lines are."*


## 5. Changes in Open Interest

The absolute level of open interest at any strike matters, but the *change* in open interest over a session is more informative.

Consider a strike where open interest is increasing while the premium is declining. This means new options are being written at that level — sellers are adding supply. The market expects the index not to reach that strike. Conversely, rising open interest with rising premium means buyers are entering — the market is betting the strike will be breached.

> *"If open interest is rising at 17,500 put but the premium is falling, somebody is selling puts. If the premium is also rising, somebody is buying them. The combination tells you who is aggressive."*

The volume-to-OI-change ratio adds another dimension: high volume with low OI change suggests intraday churn — day traders closing and reopening positions. High volume with high OI change suggests genuine new positions being built. The trader who watches both knows whether the market's positioning is being reinforced or merely refreshed.

---

### Key Takeaways

1. The max-pain strike (highest combined call and put OI) is where the market tends to gravitate in the final week before expiry. The professional writing community defends this level through hedging flows.

2. Breakeven support (strike minus premium) of the highest-OI strikes defines the market's true expected range — more reliable than chart-based support/resistance.

3. Positioning asymmetry — where one side has far more OI than the other — predicts resolution toward the crowded side's direction.

4. PCR at specific strikes within 2-3% of the market gives a cleaner signal than the aggregate PCR.

5. Changes in OI (rising premium + rising OI = buyers active; falling premium + rising OI = sellers active) identify who is building positions at each level.
