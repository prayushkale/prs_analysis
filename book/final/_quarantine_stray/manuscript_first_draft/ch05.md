# Chapter 5: Volatility Is a Regime, Not a Number

Market participants talk about volatility as if it is a problem — something to avoid, a risk to be hedged away. In reality, **volatility is a regime,** and understanding which regime is active is the single most important input for deciding what strategy to use.

India VIX — the implied volatility index of the Nifty 50 — has a well-documented range. It can be as low as 8-9 (as it was in mid-2023, a 12-year low) and as high as 85-90 (as it was during the COVID crash of March 2020). Between these extremes lies the trader's roadmap.


## 1. The Three Regimes

**Low volatility (VIX < 15):** The market is comfortable. Options are cheap. Short premium strategies — selling straddles and strangles — produce modest but reliable returns. The risk is that a sudden event (a war, a budget surprise, an unprecedented pandemic) wilts the smile from the market's face in a single session, and the cheap options explode in value against the seller.

**Moderate volatility (VIX 15-30):** The normal range for Indian markets, covering perhaps 70% of all trading days since 2010. Option premiums are high enough to make selling attractive and not so high that a hedge cannot be purchased at a reasonable price. The full range of strategies — straddles, ratio spreads, calendars, butterflies — are viable within this band.

**High volatility (VIX > 30):** Panic territory. Options are expensive. The buyer is paying a premium that implies a move the market may not deliver — but if it does, the buyer wins big. The seller, meanwhile, collects extraordinary premium but must have the capital and nerve to survive a sudden gap in either direction. This is the regime where the largest profits and the largest losses are made.

> *"When VIX is below 15, we are in low volatility. Below 15 means options are cheap. At that time, never run pure naked shorts. Pair buys with sells so that a VIX spike cushions both sides."*


## 2. VIX on Red Days

One of the most useful signals in this corpus is the VIX-verses-direction divergence. Consider a day when the market falls 200 points. Most traders assume the VIX will spike — fear rising with the fall. If instead the VIX falls, it suggests that the market's decline is not a panic but an orderly repricing. Option writers are not scrambling to cover; institutional buying is absorbing the selling.

> *"VIX falling while the market falls equals limited downside. A falling VIX on a red day is a floor signal."*

Conversely, a market that rises while the VIX rises signals that the move is being driven by aggressive call buying rather than broad-based institutional accumulation. Short call writers will be squeezed, and a sharp reversal is more likely.

This divergence signal — the direction of volatility relative to the direction of price — is more actionable than either signal alone. It tells you not just what is happening but how the market's participants are positioned.


## 3. VIX Curve and Expiry Structure

India VIX is calculated from the Nifty 50 option chain, primarily from near-month and next-month contracts. Its level is an average of implied volatilities across a range of strike prices, weighted by open interest. This means the VIX can be decomposed into a curve — the implied volatility at different expiry months.

When the near-month VIX is higher than the next-month VIX, the market is pricing short-term fear — an event that the market expects to resolve quickly. This was the pattern before every budget, every election, and every RBI policy meeting in the corpus. The curve inverts (near-month higher than next-month), and the inversion resolves into a collapse once the event passes.

When the next-month VIX is higher than the near-month, the market is pricing persistent uncertainty — a war, a tariff regime, an election whose aftermath is unpredictable. The VIX curve stays elevated across all expiries, and short premium strategies that rely on time decay do not work well because theta is offset by rising IV in later months.

> *"Usually VIX moves because option premiums move. But nowadays, the VIX moves first and option premiums follow. That raises the question: is there manipulation? Let us forget that. The signal is the same either way."*


## 4. The Collapsing-VIX Trade

The most profitable single trade structure in this corpus is the one that benefits from a collapsing VIX. Consider the trajectory of India VIX from its COVID peak of 86 in March 2020 to its low of 11 in July 2023: a fall of 75 points over roughly 40 months, punctuated by sharp spikes for every subsequent news event.

A seller who sold put options during the April 2020 panic — when VIX was 86 and put premiums were pricing catastrophe — and held them through the VIX collapse was not predicting the market's recovery. They were selling overpriced insurance into a crowd that was terrified. The VIX collapse did the work.

The same structure repeated in attenuated form during every subsequent crisis: the 2022 Fed rate shock, the 2024 election uncertainty, the 2025 tariff escalation. In each case, VIX spiked to 25-35, put premiums were bid up, and the trader who sold the overpriced insurance — or bought a longer-dated hedge and sold the front-month premium — profited from the VIX mean reversion.

> *"In high-VIX regimes, the option buyer is paying for a move that probably will not happen. The seller collects the premium. Of course, the move can happen. That is the risk. But the probability is with the seller."*


## 5. Reading Premium Behaviour

Beyond the VIX index itself, the behaviour of option premiums at specific strikes gives real-time signals about market direction.

When the market is rallying and call premiums are expanding faster than put premiums, it signals genuine bullish sentiment — buyers are chasing the move. When put premiums refuse to decline despite a rising market, it signals that participants still expect a reversal — and the rally is likely to be capped by persistent hedging demand.

In mid-2024, a pattern emerged that appeared frequently in the weekly commentaries: the market was closing in positive territory, but the India VIX was drifting higher. Overnight, put premiums did not decay as they normally would on a positive close. The market was rising, but the hedging demand was still present, meaning the rally was not trusted by the option-writing community. This divergence often preceded a sharp reversal.

> *"When call premiums shoot up 10 rupees and put premiums come down by 1 rupee — that tells you people are afraid to sell put options. They think the market can reverse any time. So put premiums stay high."*

The trader who reads this divergence can position accordingly: if puts are staying bid while the market rallies, a short call position is favoured over a short put, because the put hedge is expensive and the call side is more vulnerable to a squeeze if the rally continues.

---

### Key Takeaways

1. VIX has three regimes: low (<15), moderate (15-30), and high (>30). Each regime favours a different set of strategies. Using a strategy designed for one regime in another is the fastest way to lose capital.

2. The direction of VIX relative to the direction of the market — falling VIX on a red day, rising VIX on a green day — gives a cleaner signal than either data point alone.

3. VIX curve inversion (near-month IV higher than next-month) signals an event that the market expects to resolve quickly. A flat or upward-sloping curve signals persistent uncertainty.

4. The collapsing-VIX trade — selling overpriced insurance into panic and riding the volatility mean-reversion — is the most consistently profitable theme across the eight years of data.

5. Premium behaviour at specific strikes (puts staying bid during rallies, calls expanding faster than fundamentals justify) provides real-time positioning intelligence that the VIX index alone does not capture.
