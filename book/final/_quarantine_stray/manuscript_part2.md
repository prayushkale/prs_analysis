# Part II: How This Market Works

## Chapter 4 — The Engine Room: FII/DII Flows

### The Two Giants

The Indian equity market is, in its simplest form, a battle between two forms of capital: Foreign Institutional Investors (FIIs) and Domestic Institutional Investors (DIIs). The daily net figure — "FIIs sold ₹3,400 crore; DIIs bought ₹3,600 crore" — is the opening headline of almost every pre-market and post-market report in this entire corpus, spanning eight years.

Understanding that tug-of-war matters more than any chart pattern. Because when you know who is selling and who is buying, you know whether the day's move is structural or mechanical.

### The Declining Significance of FIIs

One of the most striking observations across the 2018–2026 corpus is the **declining marginal significance of FII flows**. In 2018, when FIIs sold ₹30,000 crore in a single month (their highest since January 2008), Nifty fell more than 10%. By 2021, FIIs sold ₹20,000 crore in October, yet Nifty went from 14,000 to 18,000 over the same six months. By 2024, monthly FII selling of ₹1 lakh crore+ was absorbed with Nifty corrections of 5–8%, not a crash. [^3]

What changed? Domestic money. Mutual funds, insurance companies, and provident funds grew larger every year. By the mid-2020s, DIIs were buying ₹8,000–10,000 crore on some days — enough to absorb any single day of FII selling. The block-deal test: when Canadian pension fund CPP sold ₹6,800 crore of Kotak Bank shares in March 2022, FIIs and DIIs combined bought ₹300 crore net that day — the block was absorbed within hours. In 2018, that would have caused a 3% collapse.

What this means for a trader: **Do not overreact to headline FII numbers.** Weight them by context — a ₹10,000 crore FII sell in 2018 was a hurricane; the same number in 2024 was a monsoon. The domestic bid is structural, not tactical.

### Reading FII Data Correctly

Cash market flows are what the media reports. But the real signal is in **FII derivatives positioning**: the index futures long-short ratio. When FIIs are above 70% long in index futures, the market is at risk of a reversal. When they are below 30% long (above 70% short), it has historically been a contrarian buy signal — because FIIs have been wrong at extremes.

"Do not worry when FIIs are selling every day," our narrator said multiple times. "Worry when they stop selling and start buying — because that means they have found a reason to come back, and that reason is usually a market bottom."

### The Block Deal Clues

Large block deals — where a major shareholder sells a substantial stake in one go — are revealing. The traders who bought in the pre-market block can sell into the retail demand that follows. If the stock holds above the block price, it signals genuine demand. If it falls below, the block was a distribution. This pattern was visible repeatedly: HDFC Bank MSCI rebalancing moves (August 2024), Kotak's CPP exit (March 2022), and the Adani FPO discount (January 2023).

### Amateurs Open, Professionals Close

"Amateurs open the market, professionals close it." This aphorism recurred through hundreds of daily reports. The logic: retail traders place their orders early (opening bell) based on overnight news and gap analysis. Professional traders wait until the afternoon to assess the true direction. Therefore:
- A big move in the first hour that **fades into the close** → professionals are selling the amateur rally → bearish signal.
- A gap-down that **recovers into the close** → professionals are buying the amateur panic → bullish signal.
- A market that opens flat and goes nowhere → no consensus → trade the range, not the breakout.

The last half hour of the trading day, between 3:00 and 3:30 PM (or 3:15–3:30 on expiry days), is where institutional money leaves its real footprint. "Any big move in the last half hour — that is real," said Dhanraj. "That move will be followed the next day."

## Chapter 5 — Volatility Is a Regime, Not a Number

### The Three Regimes

India VIX — the volatility index — is not just a "fear gauge." It is a **strategy selector**. The level of VIX tells you which strategy to use, not whether the market is going up or down.

| VIX Range | Regime | Strategy |
|-----------|--------|----------|
| Below 15 | Low volatility trap | Do NOT sell naked options; use ratio spreads and calendars |
| 15–30 | Normal | Short straddles and strangles work with proper hedging |
| Above 30 | Panic / Opportunity | Sell premium aggressively; buy protection for tail risk |
| Above 85 | Crisis (2008, 2020) | Maximum opportunity for option sellers; highest IV ever seen |

Our narrator identified a critical pattern: **"When VIX is falling while the market is falling, the downside is limited."** In September 2022, VIX fell from 30 to 20 while Nifty dropped from 18,000 to 17,000. That combination — price down, fear down — signaled absorption, not capitulation. The market bottomed within two weeks.

Conversely: "When VIX is rising while the market is flat, something is wrong." October 2023 saw VIX spike from 13 to 18 while Nifty barely moved. That divergence preceded the October 2024 correction.

### The 12-Year Low

In July 2023, India VIX touched 11.5 — a multi-year low. This was the most dangerous regime for option sellers. "At 11.5 VIX," Dhanraj warned, "you cannot run pure naked shorts. You must pair buys with sells. You must do ratio spreads. Because the moment VIX rises from 11 to 18 — even if Nifty goes nowhere — your short options will double in price."

That is the low-volatility trap: the premiums are cheap, the decay is slow, and a single volatility spike wipes out months of premium collection. The professional response is always the same: **pair every sell with a buy**, even if it reduces the credit. In a ratio spread or calendar, a VIX spike benefits the long leg as much as it harms the short leg.

### VIX Curve Inversion

When short-term VIX is higher than long-term VIX — a curve inversion — it signals that immediate panic is priced in but longer-term stability is expected. This is the ideal entry point for **calendar spreads**: sell the near-month (high IV) and buy the far-month (low IV). The near-month option decays faster than the far-month option, and the inversion gives you a structural edge.

"When the VIX curve inverts, you know the panic is priced in for today, but tomorrow will be calmer. That is when time works for you."

## Chapter 6 — Reading the Option Chain

### The Crowd Consensus

The option chain — the list of open interest (OI) at each strike price — is the most immediate gauge of where the market's money is positioned. At its simplest: the strike with the **highest put OI** is where the most sellers think the market will not fall below. The strike with the **highest call OI** is where they think it will not rise above.

But this is not a support/resistance band — at least, not directly. "Breakeven is not the strike," Dhanraj explained repeatedly. "Breakeven is the strike minus the premium collected. If I have sold the 24,000 put for ₹100, my breakeven is not 24,000. It is 23,900. I have a hundred-point cushion."

That cushion is critical. A put seller who sold 24,000 for ₹100 does not panic when Nifty hits 23,970 — they still have ₹30 of premium cushion (300 points × ₹0.10 per point = ₹30, but in Nifty options, each point is ₹25 (before the lot size change) → breakeven math varies by lot size). The actual breakeven formula: **Seller's breakeven = strike − premium** (for puts) and **strike + premium** (for calls).

### Positioning Asymmetry

"Look at what is over-sold and what is over-bought in the option chain," Dhanraj advised. "When everyone has written 18,000 calls and nobody has written puts, the market has a natural upside bias. Because the only way the call writers win is if the market stays down. The put writers are standing aside. So the pressure is upward."

This asymmetry is more reliable than any directional view. A market with heavy call OI and light put OI is like a heavily loaded upside — it naturally wants to drift higher because more participants are incentivized to push it there. Conversely, when puts are heavily written and calls are light, the gravity is downward.

### PCR: The Put-Call Ratio Misunderstood

The put-call ratio (PCR) is widely followed but widely misunderstood. A high PCR (more puts traded than calls) is often read as "bearish." Our narrator read it differently: "If everyone is buying puts, someone is selling them. Who is the seller? The professional who thinks the market will not fall. A high PCR is contrarian bullish — it means protection demand is expensive, and the sellers of that protection are the ones with capital."

A low PCR (more calls than puts) → bullish euphoria → contrarian bearish.

The key is to look at **strike-specific PCR**, not the index-level ratio. If 25,000 puts have heavy OI but 24,000 puts are empty, the real "put wall" is at 25,000, not 24,000. The market respects the strike where sellers have committed capital, not the round number.

### OI as a Leading Indicator

"OI changes before price moves," Dhanraj noted from years of tape reading. "If the OI at a strike is building up rapidly but price has not reached it yet, the market will be drawn toward that strike. It is like gravity. The options are written, the delta hedging needs price to move there."

This is particularly visible before monthly expiry: the max OI strike on the weekly or monthly chain acts as a magnetic level. Markets that are below the max OI call will drift toward it; markets above the max OI put will grind down toward it.

## Chapter 7 — Microstructure: Gaps, Expiries, and the Last Half Hour

### The Gap Taxonomy

Every day begins with a gap — an opening price different from the previous day's close. The gap's size tells you who is in control:

| Gap Size | Likely Resolution | Strategy |
|----------|-------------------|----------|
| 0–50 points (flat) | Nearly random; high volatility day | Most dangerous — either direction is possible |
| 50–150 points | Usually filled within 2–3 sessions | Mean-reverting — bet on gap filling |
| 150–300 points | Event-driven; 50% fill, 50% trend | Trade the direction, not the gap |
| 300+ points | Black swan / crash / breakout | Do not fade; wait for stabilization |

The rule-of-thumb: **Traders should never fade a big gap on day one. Wait for day two.** If the gap-up opens and closes at the highs, day two will likely be higher. If it closes lower, the gap was a liquidity grab.

### Expiry Anatomy

The weekly expiry system (which India adopted from the early 2020s) created a unique behavioral pattern: every Wednesday (Nifty), Tuesday (FinNifty), and Thursday (Bank Nifty), the market enters a "spike zone" for the final hour. Key expiry observations from the corpus:

- **Expiry day often reverses the week's trend.** If Monday–Tuesday were bullish, Wednesday (Nifty expiry) often sees profit booking. If Monday–Tuesday were bearish, Thursday (Bank Nifty expiry) often sees short covering.
- **The last hour of expiry is dominated by gamma.** Options that were ₹5 can become ₹50 or zero within minutes. Trading positions into the last hour of expiry without understanding gamma exposure is dangerous.
- **Max OI strikes tend to be the closing level.** On weekly expiry, the market often converges to the strike with the highest OI — because the sellers delta-hedge toward that level.

### Index Construction Effects

Broader market structures — not just price — create mechanical flows. When the Bank Nifty minimum constituent count was raised from 12 to 14 in October 2025, with a 20% single-stock cap and 45% top-3 cap, ETFs were forced to rebalance. HDFC Bank, which had ~30% weight, had to be reduced to 20%. ICICI Bank from ~23% to within the cap. SBI from ~10% had minimal change.

This caused weeks of mechanical selling pressure on HDFC Bank — not because anything was wrong with the bank, but because the rules changed. "Index methodology changes," Dhanraj said, "create forced flows that are more reliable than any earning season trade. If you understand the rules, you can position for the rebalance."

### The Read-Through Trade

Accenture's quarterly results — an Irish-domiciled IT services company — were a reliable predictor of Indian IT stocks for years. "Same people, same mindset are running TCS, Infosys, and Accenture," Dhanraj explained. "When Accenture reports good numbers, Indian IT opens higher the next day. Not sometimes. Every time."

This pattern extended to other sectors: good results from US banks lifted Indian private banks. Weakness in US auto sales foreshadowed weakness in Indian auto stocks. The read-through trade is one of the simplest edges in the market — it depends on the fact that Indian and US companies operating in the same sector face the same demand drivers.
