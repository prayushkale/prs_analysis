# Chapter 30: The Hard Assets Debate — Real Estate, Gold, and Bonds

Every trader or investor eventually faces the question: should I put money into hard assets — real estate, gold, bonds — or keep everything in equities and derivatives? The answer changes with the market cycle, but the underlying math has been surprisingly consistent across the years covered by this book.

## Real Estate: The Yield Test

The most persistent myth in Indian household finance is that real estate is a can't-lose investment. The numbers tell a different story. Across India's major cities between 2018 and 2026, gross rental yields averaged 2–3% of property value per year. At the same time, home loan interest rates ran at 8–9%. A landlord paying 9% interest to earn 2.5% rent is bleeding 6.5% of the property value every year — and betting entirely on price appreciation to make up the gap.

Price appreciation did occur in some pockets. The IT-corridor boom around one South Indian city's arterial road in the early 2010s, for instance, saw land values multiply several times. But a boom that attracts every retail buyer is usually closer to its end than its beginning. By the time an office clerk, a bank manager, and a cab driver all talk about "the property that never goes down," the easy money has been made.

Real estate also carries liquidity and fraud risks that financial assets do not. A buyer in an under-construction project may wait years for possession; in the worst cases, the builder converts promised amenities (a clubhouse, a park) into commercial property, local authorities seize the land, and the buyer is locked in legal limbo. Title-chain disputes — where a piece of land is found to have been sold ten or fifteen times without valid transfer — are common enough that verifying ownership through the sub-registrar's records is a mandatory step that many buyers skip.

The solution for investors who want real estate exposure without the headaches is the Real Estate Investment Trust (REIT) or Infrastructure Investment Trust (InvIT). These listed instruments hold rent-bearing commercial properties or infrastructure assets, pay out 90%+ of their income as dividends, and trade on the exchange like stocks — with full liquidity, no title risk, and a single-pan-India diversification.

## Gold: Crisis Insurance, Not Wealth Creation

Gold's role in a portfolio is best understood by looking at its three major spikes of the modern era. The first came in 1980, triggered by the Iranian revolution and the oil crisis. The second followed the 2008 Lehman collapse. The third accompanied the COVID crash of 2020.

In each case, the trigger was the same: a global crisis forced central banks to slash interest rates to near zero. When deposit rates fall below inflation, a yieldless asset like gold suddenly becomes attractive — not because it produces anything, but because the alternative (cash in the bank) is producing even less. As a market observer once put it: "Whenever there is a problem faced by the entire world, gold prices will shoot up."

Gold did not underperform dramatically during the period covered by this book. Its returns from 2018 through 2026 roughly matched the Nifty's total return during the same stretch when measured in rupee terms — but with far lower volatility. The practical takeaway: gold belongs in a portfolio as crisis insurance, not as a growth engine. An allocation of 5–10% acts as a hedge against geopolitical shocks and currency depreciation, but expecting it to outperform equities over decades has been a losing bet across every twenty-year window since 1971.

India's policy environment around gold also matters. In May 2026, the government raised the import duty on gold and silver from 6% to 15% to shore up foreign-exchange reserves. Such moves increase the effective cost of holding physical gold and widen the gap between international and domestic prices. Sovereign Gold Bonds (SGBs), which pay a small coupon and are exempt from capital-gains tax on indexation for long-term holders, mitigate some of this friction — but they too are subject to the same macro drivers.

## Bonds, Covered Bonds, and Fixed Income

The fixed-income universe in India offers a range of choices that many equity-focused participants ignore. Bank fixed deposits, the default choice, are safe (insured up to ₹5 lakh by the DICGC) but barely beat inflation post-tax — a 7% FD yields roughly 4.5–5% after tax for someone in the 30% slab, which after 4–5% inflation may be zero or negative in real terms.

Corporate bonds offer higher yields (8–10% for AA-rated issuers during normal conditions) but carry credit risk — as the Franklin Templeton debt-fund crisis of April 2020 showed. When six of its schemes worth roughly ₹30,000 crore were wound up after redemption pressure, the event cascaded into the options market because many large option sellers had pledged those debt funds as collateral. Exchanges immediately increased the collateral haircut to 100%, forcing forced liquidations that amplified the selling pressure. The lesson: in fixed income, chase yield only if you understand the credit risk — and never put all your collateral in one issuer or fund structure.

A simpler alternative is Bharat Bond, a debt ETF launched by Edelweiss that holds only AAA-rated PSU bonds maturing before the scheme's target date. At ~0.0005% expense ratio it is among India's cheapest fund products, offers yields in the 7–8% range, and — critically for a trader — can be pledged as collateral with a haircut of only ~5%. In a world where every basis point of return matters, a near-risk-free 7.5% yield on idle margin money is not trivial.

Covered bonds, introduced in India during this period, add an extra layer of security by ring-fencing a pool of assets that backs the bond — so even if the issuer defaults, the covered pool remains available to bondholders. They are worth a look for the fixed-income portion of any significant capital.

> **Key Takeaways**
> * Real estate in India fails the yield test: 2–3% rent vs 8–9% loan cost. Without price appreciation, the arithmetic does not work.
> * Title-chain fraud and construction delays are real risks. Use REITs for liquid real estate exposure.
> * Gold is crisis insurance, not a growth asset. Its spikes coincide with global rate cuts; it should never dominate a portfolio.
> * Bharat Bond ETFs offer 7–8% near-risk-free yield with a 5% collateral haircut — the best parking spot for idle option-selling margin.
> * Debt-fund collateral can be frozen overnight (as the Franklin episode showed). Diversify your collateral across structures.
