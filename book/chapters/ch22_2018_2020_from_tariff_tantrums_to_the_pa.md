# Chapter 22: 2018–2020 – From Tariff Tantrums to the Pandemic

## The Crack That No One Saw Coming

On the morning of September 21, 2018, something broke in India's financial system. It didn't make a loud noise. There was no dramatic collapse, no trading halt, no frantic news flash that stopped the nation. But in the offices of mutual fund managers, treasury desks, and NBFC boardrooms across the country, a quiet panic had already begun.

Infrastructure Leasing & Financial Services—IL&FS—had defaulted on its commercial paper obligations. The amount was ₹91 crore. Small, in the grand scheme of things. But it was the *kind* of default that mattered. IL&FS wasn't some fly-by-night operator. It was a AAA-rated infrastructure financing company, backed by some of India's largest public sector institutions. If IL&FS could default, anyone could.

I remember sitting in my office that week, watching the news channels scramble to explain what was happening. The anchors kept using the phrase "Lehman moment." It was lazy journalism, but it wasn't entirely wrong. In 2008, Lehman Brothers had been a systemically important institution whose collapse froze global credit markets. IL&FS was India's version—smaller in scale, but identical in mechanism.

Here's what most people didn't understand at the time: IL&FS wasn't just a company that had borrowed too much. It was a *shadow bank*—an institution that did banking-like activities without being regulated like a bank. It had borrowed short-term money from mutual funds and used that money to fund long-term infrastructure projects. This is called a maturity mismatch. It works beautifully when money is flowing. It kills you when the music stops.

The music stopped on September 21.

## The Anatomy of a Liquidity Freeze

To understand what happened next, you need to understand how India's non-banking financial companies (NBFCs) operated in 2018. They were the engines of credit growth that banks had abandoned. After the 2015–2017 NPA cleanup, public sector banks were too wounded to lend. NBFCs stepped into the gap. They borrowed from mutual funds, insurance companies, and pension funds—short-term money at low rates—and lent it out to real estate developers, small businesses, and vehicle buyers at higher rates.

The spread between their borrowing cost and lending rate was their profit. It worked as long as they could keep rolling over their short-term debt.

IL&FS broke that assumption.

When IL&FS defaulted, every mutual fund that held IL&FS paper had to mark down its value. That triggered redemption requests from investors who didn't want to be stuck in a fund holding toxic assets. To meet those redemptions, mutual funds had to sell whatever they could—including perfectly good NBFC debt. That selling pressure pushed down the prices of all NBFC bonds. Suddenly, every NBFC in the country faced a problem: their existing debt was losing value, and new debt was becoming impossible to raise.

This is the contagion mechanism. It's not about guilt or innocence. It's about *perception*. Once the market believes that NBFCs are risky, it treats all NBFCs as risky, regardless of their individual balance sheets. The good ones get punished alongside the bad ones.

Let me give you a worked example to make this concrete.

**The Numbers Game**

Imagine an NBFC called "SafeLend" with a perfectly healthy balance sheet. It has borrowed ₹1,000 crore from mutual funds at 8% interest, and lent that money out at 12% to small businesses. Its net interest margin is 4%—healthy enough.

Now IL&FS defaults. Mutual fund managers panic. They stop rolling over SafeLend's commercial paper when it matures. SafeLend has to repay ₹100 crore that it doesn't have in cash. It approaches a bank for a loan. The bank, now wary of all NBFCs, offers money at 14% instead of the usual 10%. SafeLend's borrowing cost jumps. Its margin collapses from 4% to 2%. It stops lending to new customers. Small businesses that depended on SafeLend for working capital suddenly have no access to credit. They slow down operations. Some shut down.

This is how a liquidity freeze becomes an economic slowdown. It's not a crash. It's a slow strangulation.

## The Yes Bank Wound

The IL&FS crisis was still festering when the next domino fell. Yes Bank had been one of India's most aggressive lenders, growing at breakneck speed through the 2010s. It had lent heavily to the same real estate and infrastructure sectors that were now under stress. By early 2020, the bank was bleeding deposits. Customers were pulling out money, spooked by the NBFC crisis and rumors about Yes Bank's own exposure.

On March 5, 2020, the Reserve Bank of India stepped in. It imposed a moratorium on Yes Bank, capping withdrawals at ₹50,000 per depositor. The stock crashed. The bank's bondholders faced losses. The entire banking system felt the shock.

This was different from IL&FS. Yes Bank was a *bank*—insured by the Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation. If a bank fails, depositors are protected up to ₹5 lakh (the limit at that time). But the *process* of failure is messy. The moratorium froze money for weeks. Businesses couldn't pay salaries. Individuals couldn't access their savings. The human cost was real.

The RBI and the government moved quickly. A reconstruction scheme was announced. State Bank of India led a rescue, taking a 48% stake in the reconstructed Yes Bank. The depositors were saved. The bondholders took a haircut. The bank survived, but its shareholders were nearly wiped out.

Here's the lesson that many investors missed: a bank rescue is not a free pass. It protects depositors, not equity holders. If you own bank stocks, you are not a depositor. You are a risk-taker. When a bank fails, equity gets destroyed first.

## The Pandemic: A Different Kind of Crash

The Yes Bank moratorium was still in effect when the world changed.

On March 11, 2020, the World Health Organization declared COVID-19 a pandemic. India went into a nationwide lockdown on March 24. The stock market had already begun its descent. The Nifty fell from 12,200 in January to 7,500 in March—a drop of nearly 38% in two months. The Sensex hit 25,638 on March 23.

This was not a financial crisis. It was a *biological* crisis that triggered an economic stop. Entire industries—travel, hospitality, retail, entertainment—simply ceased to exist overnight. Companies that had been profitable in February were fighting for survival in April.

I recall watching the trading screen on March 23, 2020. Circuit breakers were triggered repeatedly. The market would open, crash, halt, reopen, crash again. It was mechanical, almost robotic. There was no panic selling in the traditional sense—just a relentless, orderly decline as every participant tried to get out at the same time.

The difference between this crash and 2008 was the speed of the policy response. In 2008, governments took months to act. In 2020, they took days.

## The Monetary Revolution

On March 27, 2020, the government announced the Pradhan Mantri Garib Kalyan Yojana (PMGKY)—a ₹1.7 lakh crore package focused on direct transfers, free food grains, and LPG cylinders for the poor. This was followed by the Atma Nirbhar Bharat package in May, totaling ₹20 lakh crore in fiscal measures.

But the real revolution happened at the Reserve Bank of India.

The RBI cut the repo rate from 5.15% in February to 4.0% by May. It announced a moratorium on loan repayments—borrowers could defer payments for three months, later extended to six months. It conducted open market operations to inject liquidity. It launched a targeted long-term repo operation (TLTRO) to push banks to lend to specific sectors.

Most importantly, the RBI signaled that it would do *whatever it takes*. This phrase, borrowed from Mario Draghi's 2012 speech that saved the euro, became the guiding principle of Indian monetary policy in 2020.

Here's what happened in plain numbers:

- **Liquidity injection:** The RBI injected over ₹3 lakh crore into the banking system through various operations.
- **Rate cuts:** 115 basis points of rate cuts in two months.
- **Loan moratorium:** ₹8.5 lakh crore of loans were under moratorium at its peak.
- **Asset quality forbearance:** Banks were allowed to not classify loans as NPAs if they were restructured.

This was unprecedented. The RBI had never moved this fast or this aggressively. The central bank had learned from 2008: when the system is in danger of freezing, you flood it with liquidity. You don't worry about inflation. You don't worry about moral hazard. You worry about survival.

## The First Signs of Retail Entry

Something strange happened during the lockdown. While the economy was shut down and millions were losing their jobs, a new type of investor began opening demat accounts.

In March 2020, Zerodha reported opening 50,000 accounts in a single month—a record for them. By June, the number had doubled. By December, India's total demat accounts had crossed 40 million, up from 36 million at the start of the year.

Who were these new investors? They were young, first-time traders stuck at home with stimulus money in their bank accounts and nothing to do. They had watched the market crash and believed it would recover. They were right.

The Nifty bottomed at 7,500 on March 23. By June, it had recovered to 10,000. By December, it was at 14,000. The V-shaped recovery that economists had debated was happening in real time.

But here's the uncomfortable truth that I want you to remember: the recovery was not driven by fundamentals. The economy was still in shambles. GDP had contracted by 23.9% in the June quarter. Corporate earnings were destroyed. The recovery was driven by *liquidity*—the massive infusion of money from central banks and governments around the world.

When money is cheap and abundant, it flows into assets. Stocks, real estate, gold, crypto—everything rises. This is not a sign of economic health. It is a sign of monetary expansion.

## What the Market Taught Us

The period from 2018 to 2020 was a masterclass in market mechanics. Let me distill it into lessons that will serve you for a lifetime.

**First, liquidity is more important than solvency.** A solvent company can fail if it can't access cash. IL&FS was technically solvent—its assets exceeded its liabilities. But it couldn't roll over its debt, so it defaulted. This is why cash flow analysis matters more than balance sheet analysis in times of stress.

**Second, contagion is irrational but real.** When IL&FS defaulted, the market punished all NBFCs, not just the weak ones. When Yes Bank failed, the market punished all private banks. This is not fair, but it is how markets work. You cannot fight the crowd. You can only position yourself to survive the stampede.

**Third, central banks have learned to act fast.** The 2020 response was dramatically faster than 2008. The RBI and the government understood that in a liquidity crisis, speed is everything. Delay turns a liquidity problem into a solvency problem.

**Fourth, retail investors enter at the bottom.** The surge in demat accounts in March–June 2020 was not a coincidence. First-time investors are often the last to panic and the first to buy. They lack the experience to be terrified. This can be an advantage, but it can also be dangerous when the easy money stops flowing.

**Fifth, the V-shaped recovery was a liquidity event, not a fundamental one.** The market recovered because money was printed, not because the economy healed. This distinction matters because it tells you what to watch in the future: when liquidity dries up, the party ends.

## The Bridge to 2021

As 2020 drew to a close, the market was at an all-time high. The Nifty had recovered from 7,500 to 14,000. The Sensex was above 47,000. Retail investors were euphoric. IPOs were being oversubscribed. The "new India" narrative was in full swing.

But beneath the surface, the cracks remained. The NBFC sector was still wounded. Bank balance sheets were still stressed. The economy was still recovering. And a new threat was emerging: inflation.

The liquidity that had saved the market in 2020 would become the source of its next crisis. But that story belongs to the next chapter.

For now, let me leave you with this: the market is a machine that processes information. In 2018, the information was that India's shadow banking system was fragile. In 2020, the information was that the world could stop overnight. The market processed both, crashed, recovered, and moved on.

Your job as an investor is not to predict the next crisis. It is to understand the mechanics of the machine so that when the next crisis comes, you are not caught off guard.

---

## Key Takeaways

1. **Liquidity crises spread faster than solvency crises.** A single default can freeze an entire sector if the market loses confidence in the mechanism of short-term borrowing.

2. **Bank rescues protect depositors, not shareholders.** When a bank fails, equity holders are wiped out first. Owning bank stocks means accepting this risk.

3. **Central bank response speed determines crash depth.** The faster the liquidity injection, the shallower the market decline. The 2020 response was historically fast.

4. **Retail investors often enter at market bottoms.** The surge in demat accounts during the COVID crash was a contrarian signal that the worst was over.

5. **V-shaped recoveries driven by liquidity are not the same as fundamental recoveries.** When the money printing stops, the market must stand on its own feet.
