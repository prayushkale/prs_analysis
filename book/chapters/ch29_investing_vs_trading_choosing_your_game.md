## Chapter 29: The Big Picture – Navigating Macro Shocks

### The Day the Screen Went Red

At 9:15 a.m. on June 4, 2024, the Nifty opened with a nervous gap down. By 11:00 a.m., it was a bloodbath. The index slid past 22,000, then 21,500. At its lowest, Nifty touched 21,281 — a drop of nearly 8.5% from the previous close. Trading terminals across India flashed red. News channels ran split screens: one side showed counting trends, the other showed the Sensex in freefall. Callers on business television used words like "massacre" and "crash." Some investors sold everything by lunchtime. Others sat frozen, watching their portfolios shrink by lakhs in a matter of hours.

Then something strange happened. The index closed at roughly 22,028 — down about 6% from the previous close, but well above the day's low. Over the following weeks, it began to climb. By late September 2024, Nifty had scaled record highs thirty-two times, reaching around 26,000. The investor who sold at 21,500 locked in a loss. The investor who held — or bought at the low — watched their portfolio recover and then grow by over 20% in a few months.

This is the first lesson of macro shocks: **the event is not the outcome. The reaction is.**

---

### What Is a Macro Shock?

A macro shock is any event that disrupts the broad economic or political landscape, forcing investors to reprice assets rapidly. Elections, budget announcements, central bank policy surprises, regulatory overhauls, geopolitical tensions, and natural disasters all qualify. These events don't move one stock or one sector; they move the entire market, because they change the assumptions on which every valuation is built.

Think of the stock market as a giant weighing scale. On one side sits the current reality of companies — their earnings, their order books, their cash flows. On the other side sits the collective expectation of what the future will look like. Macro shocks don't change today's earnings; they change expectations. And when expectations shift suddenly, the scale tips violently.

On June 4, 2024, the expectation was that the ruling alliance would return with a comfortable majority. When early counting trends suggested otherwise, the market rapidly repriced the probability of a weaker coalition government. The uncertainty was not about whether the economy would survive; it was about whether policy continuity would hold. Investors hate uncertainty more than they hate bad news. A known delay is easier to price than an unknown outcome.

This is why markets crash on election surprises, not on the substance of the surprise itself. The crash is a liquidity event, not a valuation event. It is the sound of thousands of investors asking the same question at the same time: "What happens now?" And because nobody knows, the price drops until someone feels they know enough to buy.

### The Two Investors: A Worked Example

Let's put hard numbers on the June 4, 2024 scenario. Suppose two investors, Arjun and Meera, each held ₹10 lakh in a Nifty index fund on the morning of June 4.

**Arjun** watched the morning slide with rising panic. At 11:30 a.m., when Nifty was around 21,500 — down 8.5% from the previous close of ~23,500 — he sold everything. His portfolio:

- Sale value: ₹10,00,000 × (21,500 / 23,500) = ₹9,14,894
- Realised loss: ₹85,106

Arjun decided to "wait for clarity." He kept his money in cash for the next three months. By late September, Nifty had recovered to 26,000 — around 21% above his sale price. His cash was still ₹9,14,894. To buy back into the market at 26,000 would require him to pay:

- Cost to buy back: ₹9,14,894 × (26,000 / 21,500) = ₹11,06,000

That means Arjun now needs to put in **an additional ₹1,91,106** of fresh capital just to get back what he had in January. His panic cost him not just ₹85,000 but the opportunity cost of missing one of the sharpest rallies of the year.

**Meera** also watched the crash. But she had read Dhanraj's old notes on macro shocks. She knew that election crashes rarely change the fundamental growth story of a country; they merely change the entry price. She did nothing. Her ₹10,00,000 stayed fully invested.

- Low point value: ~₹9,10,000 (at Nifty 21,281)
- Closing value (June 4): ~₹9,37,000
- Late September value (Nifty ~26,000): ₹10,00,000 × (26,000 / 23,500) = ₹11,06,000

Meera's portfolio didn't just recover; it grew by ₹1,06,000 in four months. She used no skill, no timing, no stock-picking. She simply understood that macro shocks are temporary, while economic growth is a long-run phenomenon.

Now, I was not one of those investors in June 2024. But I have seen this pattern repeat in 2004, 2009, 2014, 2019, and 2024. The lesson is always the same: **when markets panic, the only people who lose are those who are forced to sell — either by their own fear or by their financial planning.**

### Election-Day Crashes: A Historical Table

India has seen election-related market shocks before. Comparing them reveals a pattern that is almost boring in its predictability.

| Year | Event | Market Drop (approx.) | Recovery Time | Subsequent 1-Year Return |
|------|-------|----------------------|---------------|--------------------------|
| 2004 | NDA loses, UPA forms coalition | ~11% in two sessions | ~3 months | ~25% |
| 2009 | UPA wins unexpectedly strong | Positive reaction | N/A | ~75% |
| 2014 | NDA wins outright majority | Positive reaction | N/A | ~35% |
| 2024 | NDA returns with slimmer majority | ~6% on June 4, ~8.5% intraday | ~2 months to June low | ~11% from June 4 low to late Sept |

Note the pattern: when the market expected one outcome and got another, it fell sharply. When the market was surprised in a positive direction, it rallied. But in every case, the long-term driver was not the election result — it was corporate earnings, GDP growth, and policy direction. Elections are punctuation marks, not sentences.

The 2004 crash is instructive. On May 17, 2004, the Nifty fell nearly 12% in a single day after the election result surprised most pollsters. Investors who sold that day missed one of the biggest bull runs in Indian history — from around 1,300 in 2004 to over 6,000 by early 2008. The coalition government, despite fears of instability, delivered strong economic growth. The market eventually realised that governments change, but Indian businesses keep compounding.

### Coalition Governments and Market Sentiment

Why do markets dislike coalition governments? The fear is not that coalitions are inherently bad for the economy. The fear is that they lead to policy paralysis. A single-party majority government can push through unpopular but necessary reforms — land acquisition, labour codes, tax simplification. A coalition must negotiate with allies, often at the cost of economic efficiency.

But here's the part that often gets lost: **India has a long history of coalition governments that delivered solid growth.** The 1999-2004 NDA coalition, the 2004-2014 UPA coalition — both oversaw significant economic expansion. Markets initially panicked when those coalitions took power, but they ultimately flowed with the tide of earnings. The proof is in the table above: the 2004 coalition period produced a 25% one-year forward return.

The real issue is uncertainty, not composition. When the counting trends are still live, nobody knows the final arithmetic. That uncertainty creates a vacuum. Buyers pull back because they have no framework for pricing the future. Sellers step in because they want to protect bonuses or meet margin calls. The price falls to wherever the most panic meets the most liquidity. That is not a fair value; it is a temporary equilibrium.

Dhanraj once told me a simple rule: "If you can't predict an outcome, don't trade it; watch it. The market will give you a second chance. It always does. Because even after a crash, there is a reaction. And the reaction is always more rational than the crash."

He was right. After June 4, the market recovered not because the final coalition was suddenly seen as great, but because the immediate panic created an enormous gap between price and intrinsic value. Buyers stepped in to fill that gap. The recovery was not a gift from politicians; it was a consequence of arithmetic.

### Regulatory Shocks: The GST Overhaul of 2025

Elections are not the only macro shocks. Regulatory changes can be equally disruptive. Consider the GST slab overhaul that took effect in September 2025. The government shifted several goods between tax slabs. Some consumer staples — previously at 12% — moved to 5%. Some luxury items moved from 18% to 28%. And some essentials were exempted entirely.

This was not a crash event. The Nifty did not collapse. But it caused a powerful sector rotation. Companies in the consumer discretionary space, which had benefited from lower taxes on their products, suddenly faced margin pressure. Conversely, companies that made everyday essentials got a margin lift. For a few weeks, stock prices moved less on company fundamentals and more on "who wins, who loses from the GST changes."

A macro-focused investor would not panic. They would ask: "Which companies are going to see earnings changes? And are those changes already priced in?" Often, the market overreacts initially. A stock may fall 8% on a GST change when the actual margin impact is only 3%. That gap creates an opportunity.

The GST overhaul also highlights a key difference between political shocks and regulatory shocks. A political shock is about who is in power; a regulatory shock is about what the rules become. Both are macro events, but they require different analysis. For political shocks, you focus on policy continuity. For regulatory shocks, you focus on sector-level winners and losers.

### How to Assess Long-Term Impact

The most useful framework I know comes from a transcript I read years ago. The analyst said: "For any macro shock, ask three questions.

1. **Does this change the economic growth trajectory?** If the economy still grows at 6-7% GDP, the market will ultimately reflect that. If a shock reduces GDP growth to 3%, that is a different story.

2. **Does this change the earnings trajectory of companies I own?** An election result rarely changes the demand for toothpaste, cement, or software services. A GST slab change might. For each holding, estimate the actual earnings impact.

3. **Does this change the liquidity environment?** If FIIs are selling heavily, that can persist for weeks. But Indian mutual funds and retail investors now have enough domestic liquidity to absorb the selling. This is a crucial structural change since 2014."

If the answer to all three is "no" or "mild," then the correct response to a macro shock is almost always to do nothing. The market will find equilibrium. The only time you should act is if the shock permanently alters the business environment of a specific sector you are exposed to.

### The Missing Sessions Problem

One additional risk deserves mention: market holidays coinciding with macro events. In November 2021, the US Federal Reserve announced its taper decision on a day when Indian markets were closed for Diwali. When markets reopened, traders had to absorb two sessions of global price action in one. Gap risk doubled. We saw similar friction whenever a major budget or election result lands on a weekend.

How do you handle that? You size your positions so you can survive a gap. You don't use borrowed money for events where you don't know the outcome. You treat the gap as a cost of being in the market. This is not a prediction; it is preparation.

### The Deeper Game

The title of this part is **Wealth Beyond the Screen**. That phrase captures the deepest truth about macro shocks: they are visible only on screens, but they are driven by forces that have existed for centuries — power shifts, tax changes, wars, and technological disruption. The investor who watches only the screen sees noise. The investor who follows the forces behind the screen sees signal.

On June 4, 2024, the screen showed red. But behind the screen, Indian mutual funds were still receiving monthly SIP contributions. Companies were still generating revenue. Banks were still lending. The economy was still moving. The screen is only a mirror; it is not the body.

Dhanraj loved to say: "The market is a voting machine in the short run and a weighing machine in the long run." That sentence, often attributed to Benjamin Graham, is missing a third line: "In a crisis, the voting machine counts only fear." But fear has a shelf life. Eventually, the weighing machine takes over.

---

### Your Game: Investor or Trader?

This chapter sits between the trading-focused sections and the remaining chapters of this part. Here is the key distinction:

A trader must respond to macro shocks because their holding period is days. A trader without a plan for a crash will be wiped out.

An investor must respond to macro shocks only if their fundamental thesis has broken. For an investor, a crash is a re-pricing event, not a thesis event.

So when the next macro shock hits — and it will — you need to know which game you are playing.

If you are a trader, the question is: "What is my stop-loss?"  
If you are an investor, the question is: "Has my company's earning power permanently declined?"

If you try to play both games at once, you will sell your good companies on fear and buy bad companies on hope. That is how wealth is destroyed.

---

### Key Takeaways

- **Macro shocks are repricing events, not value events.** The underlying economy rarely changes in a day; the expectation does.
- **Election-day crashes in India have historically recovered within 1–3 months** and were followed by positive one-year returns. June 4, 2024 followed the same pattern.
- **Do not sell into panic unless your personal liquidity forces you to.** If you must sell for a legitimate need, do it in a planned way, not at the low.
- **For regulatory shocks, focus on sector winners and losers, not on the index.** The GST overhaul was a rotation event, not a crash.
- **Know your game.** Traders need pre-defined reactions; investors need a thesis about permanent earning power.
