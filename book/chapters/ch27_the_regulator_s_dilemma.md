# Chapter 27: The Regulator's Dilemma

## Predators & Protectors

---

**January 16, 2024. 9:15 AM.**

The market opened, and within minutes, something felt wrong. Not the usual wrong of a nervous opening or a gap-down on global cues. Something deeper. Mechanical.

HDFC Bank, India's most valuable lender by market capitalisation, began to slide. Not a gentle drift—a controlled collapse. By 9:45 AM, it was down 5%. By 11:00 AM, 8.5%. In a single morning, over ₹1 lakh crore of shareholder wealth evaporated. The stock hit its lower circuit breaker. Trading was halted.

Across trading screens, retail investors stared in disbelief. HDFC Bank was supposed to be the safe bet—the blue-chip that grandmothers held for decades. How could a bank that reported a 33% rise in quarterly profit just days earlier lose nearly a tenth of its value in hours?

The answer, as it often is in markets, was hiding in plain sight. The bank's quarterly results had revealed something unsettling: its loan-to-deposit ratio had crept to 110%. It was lending more than it had in deposits. For a bank, that's like spending more than you earn—sustainable only as long as no one asks too many questions. But more importantly, the stock had run up significantly in prior months. Institutional holders, sensing vulnerability, had been quietly reducing positions.

The 8.5% crash wasn't a scandal. No fraud was uncovered. No promoter was caught embezzling. It was something far more common and far more dangerous: **concentration risk meeting liquidity mismatch**. Too many large holders trying to exit the same stock at the same time.

Circuit breakers kicked in. The market-wide circuit breaker—triggered when the broader index falls 10%, 15%, or 20%—never activated. But the stock-level circuit breaker did its job. It stopped trading, gave the market time to breathe, and prevented a panic that could have spread to other banking stocks.

By close, Bank Nifty had fallen entirely due to HDFC Bank's drag. Other banking stocks were largely flat. The circuit breaker had contained the damage.

But here's the uncomfortable truth the regulators face: **circuit breakers don't solve the underlying problem**. They delay it. When HDFC Bank reopened the next day, it fell further. The exit continued. The wealth destruction was merely spread across two days instead of one.

This is the regulator's dilemma. Every day, SEBI must balance two conflicting goals: protect investors from predators, and allow markets to function freely. Too much protection, and markets become rigid. Too little, and predators feast.

## The Three Faces of the Regulator

To understand why regulators face such a difficult task, you must first understand that they are not fighting one enemy. They are fighting three.

### Face One: The Insider

Consider a company preparing to announce a disastrous quarterly result. The CEO knows. The CFO knows. The company secretary knows.

One of them has a nephew who trades. A casual conversation at a family dinner: "The numbers aren't good. I'd sell if I were you." The nephew sells the next morning. The stock falls 15% when results are announced two weeks later. The nephew has saved his capital.

This is insider trading—the original sin of financial markets. It's also nearly impossible to prove. How do you distinguish between a tip and a hunch? Between a deliberate leak and a careless remark overheard at a wedding?

SEBI's approach has evolved from relying solely on direct evidence—phone records, emails, confessions—to using circumstantial evidence: unusual trading patterns, connections between traders and company insiders, timing coincidences that strain credibility.

In 2021, SEBI passed an order against several individuals for trading in RIL shares ahead of a major announcement. The evidence wasn't a smoking gun. It was a mosaic: phone call records showing contact between the traders and an RIL employee, trades placed just days before the announcement, profits that followed a predictable pattern.

The regulator had built a case from fragments. But such cases take years. By the time the order came, the markets had moved on.

**The dilemma:** Insider trading is the most corrosive crime in markets—it destroys the level playing field that makes retail participation possible. But to catch insiders, regulators must monitor phones, track personal relationships, and analyse patterns that could also be innocent coincidences. Every investigation is a balancing act between justice and privacy.

### Face Two: The Manipulator

In 2001, Ketan Parekh demonstrated a different kind of predation. He didn't need insider information. He created his own information.

His method was circular trading. He would buy shares of a small tech company through one set of brokers, sell them through another, and buy them back through a third. The shares never really changed hands—they simply went in circles. But to the market, it looked like genuine demand. Volume increased. The price rose.

Retail investors saw a stock moving up with high volumes. They bought in. Parekh sold.

This is the classic pump-and-dump, refined for the Indian context. Parekh used a network of brokers who were willing to look the other way, banks that provided funding against inflated collateral, and a regulatory framework that simply couldn't track ownership quickly enough.

The technology to detect circular trading exists today. SEBI's surveillance systems can flag stocks where a small group of entities account for an unusually high percentage of trading volume. They can identify "connected groups" that trade among themselves. They can freeze suspicious profits before they are withdrawn.

But manipulators evolve. Today's predators use high-frequency trading algorithms to spoof the market—placing large orders they never intend to execute, simply to create the illusion of demand. They use participatory notes and offshore structures to hide their identities. They employ shell companies that exist only on paper, registered to nominees who have no idea they are directors.

**The dilemma:** Every tool SEBI develops to catch manipulators can also be used by manipulators to hide more effectively. The regulator is always one step behind, because predators don't have to follow rules of procedure. They don't need to publish draft regulations for public comment. They don't need to justify their actions to parliamentary committees.

### Face Three: The Crowd

On June 4, 2024, the market faced a different kind of threat. Lok Sabha election results were being counted, and early trends suggested the ruling party might fall short of expectations.

By 10:00 AM, the Nifty was down 5%. By noon, 8.5%. The index touched 21,281—a level not seen in months. Panic selling had begun.

But here's what's fascinating: there was no predator involved. No one was manipulating the market. No insider was trading on secret information. The selling was entirely rational—investors adjusting their expectations to a new political reality.

Yet the selling was irrational in its intensity. The difference between an 8% fall and a 12% fall wasn't driven by fundamentals. It was driven by fear. By margin calls. By one investor selling because they saw another investor selling.

This is when the regulator's job shifts from protecting investors from predators to protecting investors from themselves. The circuit breaker mechanism—if the Nifty falls 10%, trading halts for 45 minutes—is designed for precisely this moment. It forces a pause. It lets rationality reassert itself.

On June 4, the market never hit that 10% threshold. The Nifty closed at 21,884, down about 5.9% from the previous close. It had recovered from its worst levels. By the next day, the recovery continued. Within months, the market hit record highs.

"The best regulation," Dhanraj once told me, "is the regulation that never needs to be used. A circuit breaker that never triggers is not a failure. It is a success. Because it means the market self-corrected before the safety net was needed."

## The Tools of Protection

How does SEBI actually protect investors? Let me walk you through the key tools, as they actually function—not as the theory books describe them.

### Circuit Breakers

There are two types of circuit breakers in Indian markets.

**Market-wide circuit breakers** are triggered when the Sensex or Nifty falls by certain thresholds:
- 10%: Trading halts for 45 minutes (if before 1:00 PM) or 15 minutes (if after).
- 15%: Trading halts for 1 hour 45 minutes.
- 20%: Trading halts for the remainder of the day.

These have been triggered only a handful of times in Indian history: during the 2008 financial crisis, during the COVID crash of March 2020, and on a few other occasions.

**Stock-level circuit breakers** are triggered when an individual stock moves beyond a certain percentage. The range depends on the stock's liquidity and volatility. For most stocks, the circuit is set at 10%, 15%, or 20%. When triggered, trading in that stock stops for the remainder of the day.

Here's what most investors don't understand: circuit breakers don't prevent losses. They manage them. On January 16, 2024, HDFC Bank's circuit breaker didn't save investors from losing ₹1 lakh crore. It merely spread that loss over two days instead of one.

But that matters. Because a sudden, violent loss can trigger panic selling across the entire market. A slower, more orderly decline gives investors time to think. It gives brokers time to manage margin calls. It gives the system time to absorb the shock.

### Position Limits and Margins

Before 2021, a trader could take enormous positions in the futures and options market with relatively little capital. This was how many predators operated—using leverage to create artificial pressure on prices.

In 2021, SEBI introduced stricter position limits and margin requirements. Today, traders must put up more capital for every contract they hold. They must disclose concentrated positions earlier.

The result? Less room for manipulation. But also less liquidity. Some legitimate traders—hedgers, arbitrageurs—found their strategies less profitable.

**The trade-off:** Every rupee of margin that prevents a predator from manipulating the market is also a rupee of capital that a genuine hedger cannot deploy elsewhere.

### Surveillance and Enforcement

SEBI's Integrated Surveillance Department monitors trading in real-time. Their systems flag unusual patterns: a stock that moves 20% in a day on no news; a sudden spike in volume from a small group of traders; options positions that predict corporate announcements with suspicious accuracy.

When a flag is raised, an investigation begins. The regulator can:
- Freeze accounts holding suspicious positions
- Seize profits from trades deemed illegal
- Ban individuals from trading for years
- Refer cases for criminal prosecution

In the last decade, SEBI has passed hundreds of orders against manipulators. But the enforcement process is slow. An insider trading case can take three to five years to reach a conclusion. By then, the predator may have moved on to other markets, other countries, other victims.

## The Unspoken Tensions

Every regulatory action creates winners and losers. Understanding these tensions helps you—the retail investor—predict what the regulator might do next.

### The Midcap Mayhem of 2023

In late 2023, SEBI noticed something concerning. Mid-cap and small-cap funds were receiving record inflows. Mutual fund managers, obligated to deploy this money, were buying shares in the same stocks. Prices were rising—not because companies were more valuable, but because more money was chasing available shares.

This is how bubbles form. Not through manipulation, but through the mechanical pressure of money flowing into a limited pool of assets.

SEBI's response was unusual. Instead of issuing a formal regulation, it issued a "directive" to AMFI—the mutual fund industry body. The message was clear: slow down the inflows into mid-cap and small-cap funds. Encourage investors to understand the risks.

The market reacted immediately. Mid-cap and small-cap indices fell sharply. The Nifty closed decisively below 22,000, losing over 300 points in a single day.

Retail investors were angry. They accused SEBI of "creating panic." Some claimed the regulator was protecting large institutions at the expense of small investors.

But here's what the critics missed: SEBI's directive was not about stopping mid-cap investing. It was about slowing the momentum before it became unsustainable. The regulator had seen this movie before—in 2000, in 2007, in 2017. Every time, retail investors piled into a hot segment after the easy money had been made, and every time, they were the ones left holding the bag when the music stopped.

**The tension:** The regulator's job is to prevent bubbles. But preventing a bubble means cooling enthusiasm when it's still profitable to be enthusiastic. This makes the regulator the party-pooper—the one who warns about the fire when everyone is enjoying the warmth.

### The STT Controversy

In 2004, India introduced the Securities Transaction Tax (STT). The government's argument was simple: instead of taxing capital gains (which required complex tracking of purchase prices and holding periods), it would tax the transaction itself at a low rate.

STT was sold as a simplification. Pay a small tax on every trade, and your long-term capital gains would be tax-free. No need to maintain records for years. No need to compute indexed cost of acquisition.

The trade-off seemed fair. Traders paid STT but saved on capital gains tax.

In 2018, the government reintroduced long-term capital gains tax on equities. Gains above ₹1 lakh were now taxed at 10%. But STT remained.

This is the regulator's dilemma in tax form. The original bargain—STT in exchange for no LTCG—was broken. Now, investors pay both. And STT, unlike capital gains tax, cannot be offset against losses. It cannot be deducted. It is a tax on every transaction, profitable or not.

**The investor's question:** If the original promise was broken, why should investors trust the next promise?

I don't have a simple answer. But I can tell you this: the STT is unlikely to be abolished. It generates over ₹30,000 crore annually for the government. And governments, once they find a reliable source of revenue, rarely let it go.

### The 50% Cash Component Rule

In 2023, SEBI introduced a rule requiring brokers to collect at least 50% of the option premium in cash from buyers of out-of-the-money options. Previously, buyers could use collateral—shares, mutual funds, bank guarantees—to cover their positions.

A friend of mine, managing a portfolio worth ₹130 crore, was caught off guard. He had been selling deeply out-of-the-money options as part of a hedging strategy. The new rule meant he had to put up cash margin that tied up capital he could have deployed elsewhere.

He called me, frustrated. "This kills my strategy. I can't deploy capital as efficiently."

I understood his frustration. But I also understood SEBI's logic. The options market had exploded in size. Daily turnover in options exceeded that of the cash market by a factor of ten. Most of this volume came from retail traders buying cheap, out-of-the-money options—lottery tickets, really—with no understanding of the risks.

The regulator saw a problem: retail investors were burning capital on options that almost always expired worthless, while brokers were collecting commissions without ensuring clients understood the risks.

The 50% cash rule was designed to mitigate this. By requiring cash margin, SEBI ensured that at least half the risk was backed by actual cash—not leveraged through collateral that could be sold in a crisis.

**The tension:** Regulations designed to protect retail investors from their own worst impulses are inevitably paternalistic. They assume the regulator knows better than the investor what's good for them. In many cases, it's true. But paternalism breeds resentment.

## What This Means for You

Every chapter in this book has been leading to this point. You have learned how markets work. You have learned how to analyse companies. You have learned about risk and return. Now, you need to understand the environment in which you operate.

**Rule One: The regulator is not your friend.**

This sounds harsh, but it's accurate. The regulator's job is to maintain market integrity. Sometimes that aligns with your interests. Sometimes it doesn't. When SEBI raises margins, it may reduce your returns. When it imposes position limits, it may prevent you from hedging effectively. When it bans certain products, it may eliminate opportunities.

Understand the regulator's logic, but don't expect the regulator to protect you from your own mistakes.

**Rule Two: Scandals are predictable.**

Every major Indian market scam has followed the same pattern:
1. An asset class becomes popular (banking stocks in the 1990s, tech stocks in 2000, real estate in 2008, small-caps in 2023).
2. Prices rise faster than fundamentals justify.
3. The regulator warns, but is ignored.
4. The bubble bursts, and retail investors blame the regulator for not acting sooner.

If a stock is rising 20% per month with no change in its business, it's not a miracle. It's a setup. The question is not whether it will crash, but who will be left holding the shares when it does.

**Rule Three: Protection is expensive.**

Every regulation has a cost. Circuit breakers delay exits. Margins reduce leverage. Position limits constrain strategies. STT eats into profits.

The most protected market in the world is also the most expensive to trade in. You must decide whether the protection is worth the cost. For most long-term investors, it is. For day traders, it may not be.

## The Unresolved Question

There is one question the regulator cannot answer. It haunts every SEBI chairperson, every enforcement director, every market expert who has seen the cycle repeat:

*Are we protecting investors, or are we encouraging them to take risks they don't understand?*

Every time SEBI tightens a rule, it creates a false sense of security. Investors assume that because the regulator is watching, they don't need to. They trade options without understanding volatility. They buy small-caps without analysing balance sheets. They follow tips without questioning sources.

The ultimate protection is not a circuit breaker or a margin rule. It is an educated investor who understands that markets are not safe—they are merely regulated.

The regulator can catch the predator who manipulates a stock. But it cannot catch the predator who sells you a dream you should have known was false.

That responsibility is yours.

---

## Key Takeaways

1. **Regulation is always a trade-off.** Every protective measure—circuit breakers, margins, position limits—reduces market freedom. Understand the cost of protection before demanding more of it.

2. **Scams follow patterns, not surprises.** A stock rising without fundamental reason, a sector attracting sudden inflows, a promoter who seems too confident—these are red flags. The regulator may catch the crooks eventually, but you must avoid them first.

3. **The STT is a fact, not a debate.** India's tax regime for securities is unlikely to change meaningfully. Build your strategy around the current rules, not the rules you wish existed.

4. **Circuit breakers manage panic, they don't prevent losses.** On days of extreme volatility, your stop-loss may not execute at the price you expected. Plan accordingly.

5. **Your best regulator is yourself.**
