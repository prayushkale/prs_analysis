# Chapter 25: 2025–2026 – Tariffs, Jane Street and the New Regime

## A Market Chronicle 2018–2026

---

The first sign that something fundamental had changed came on a Tuesday in early April 2025, when the Nifty opened gap-down by nearly 700 points. The overnight trigger was an American tariff announcement—a reciprocal levies schedule that hit almost every trading partner, including India. But the deeper signal was not in the percentage drop. It was in what happened afterwards.

The index fell. It recovered. It fell again. It drifted sideways for the rest of the session, closing about a percent and a half down. There was no panic, no cascade of stop-losses, no frantic value-buying. Just a dull, mechanical absorption of news.

I watched the options chain that evening. The put-call ratio was elevated, but not extreme. The India VIX had spiked to 19, then settled around 16.5. And I remembered what a derivatives trader had told me three years earlier: "Wait until the algos become the market. Then you'll see what 'efficient' really means—and you won't like it."

That day, I understood.

The market had not become calmer. It had become *processed*.

## The Great Pause

To understand 2025–2026, you have to rewind to the summer of 2024. That was the moment when Indian equities lost their innocence—not in the dramatic sense of a crash, but in the slower, more corrosive sense of a plateau that refused to end.

For the 15 months from October 2024 to January 2026, the Nifty delivered a nominal return of approximately zero. Not a bear market, not a crash—a flat line with occasional heartbeats. Meanwhile, the S&P 500 rose 15–50% depending on which 15-month window you picked. In dollar terms, Indian equities gave global investors precisely nothing: no price appreciation, no currency cushion, and a rupee that slid from 82 to 91 against the dollar.

Foreign institutional investors sold approximately ₹5 lakh crore (roughly $60 billion) of Indian equities across 2023–2025. In any other emerging market, a sell-off of that magnitude would have triggered a balance-of-payments crisis. India absorbed it—largely because domestic mutual funds and retail investors kept buying every dip. But here's the uncomfortable arithmetic: if the Nifty is flat while domestic investors add 2–3 crore new demat accounts and SIP inflows hit record highs month after month, then someone is selling into that buying.

That someone was the foreign institutional complex.

Why? The conventional answer was "valuations." The Nifty traded at 20–22 times forward earnings for most of 2024–2025, while the Sensex's price-to-earnings ratio hovered at levels that made global fund managers wince. But valuations are a trailing indicator. The real answer, I believe, was more structural: India had become a market where the *rules of the game were changing faster than the players could adapt*.

This is where I need to be honest with you, because I've spent the better part of twenty years writing about Indian markets, and I've never seen a period quite like this one. It wasn't just that the market didn't go up. It was that everything that used to work stopped working—sector rotation, momentum, value, quality. Every strategy bled. The people who did well were those who traded high-frequency, had zero overnight exposure, or simply sat on cash.

That's not a market. That's a toll booth.

## Tariffs and the New Geometry of Trade

The tariff shock of 2025 was not a bolt from the blue. It was a slow-moving train that investors refused to see because they were looking at the wrong tracks.

In early 2025, the United States imposed a 25% base tariff on Indian imports, plus an additional 25% for the purchase of Russian crude oil—a penalty for what Washington interpreted as indirect financing of the Ukraine war. India's attempts to negotiate a trade deal were described by both sides as "fruitful" and "progressing." That is diplomatic code for *nothing is happening*. Diplomatic language always sounds positive when there's no resolution.

The broader point is simple: the post-2008 era of globalization was over. The 1991 liberalization that made India's growth story possible was itself a product of a particular geopolitical moment. By 2025, that moment had passed. Every country was retreating into economic nationalism. India, despite its rhetoric about becoming a manufacturing hub, remained a net importer of energy, capital goods, and—crucially—risk appetite.

Here is the brutal arithmetic that the Indian equity market finally had to confront. From 2003 to 2024, India enjoyed three distinct bull markets, each driven by a different engine:

1. **2003–2008**: Global liquidity, commodity supercycle, and the first wave of outsourcing.
2. **2013–2017**: Domestic reforms, GST anticipation, and a political mandate for change.
3. **2020–2023**: Free money, a retail trading revolution, and a zero-interest-rate world that made every equity look undervalued.

The fourth engine—the one that would power 2025 and beyond—had not been invented yet. And the market was standing in an empty station, waiting for a train that existed only in policy white papers.

I remember a conversation in August 2025 with a small-cap fund manager. His fund was down 12% for the year. "The SIPs keep coming," he said, gesturing at his screen. "But the SIPS keep selling. I'm managing a product that survives on inflows from people who have never seen a bear market. I'm the last buyer of everything, and I'm starting to feel like a bagholder."

He didn't use the word "bagholder" in the derogatory retail sense. He meant it literally. His fund was holding positions that no liquid institutional player wanted. The exit door was there, but it was bolted shut.

## The Nightmare Math of a Costly Market

Let me give you a worked example, because numbers make abstract problems concrete.

Suppose you are a retail investor with ₹10 lakh. In 2023–2024, you decided to trade index options on the Nifty. You are not a gambler—you use a sensible, backtested strategy of selling far out-of-the-money spreads. The historical win rate is about 85%. The average monthly return is 1.8%, before costs. That's an annualized return of 24%—excellent by any standard.

Now apply the actual cost structure from 2024 onwards. The Securities Transaction Tax (STT) was hiked on options sales from 0.0625% to 0.1% of the premium, and for futures it went from 0.0125% to 0.02%. The long-term capital gains tax was raised from 10% to 12.5%. The short-term rate went from 15% to 20%. The market moved from a regime where taxes were a small drag to one where they were a substantial pipeline leak.

Here's the worked example:

- Monthly premium turnover: ₹20 lakh (20 times your capital, which is modest compared to active option sellers)
- STT on options sales: 0.1% × half your turnover (sales side only) = ₹1,000
- Brokerage and exchange fees: ~₹500
- Slippage (bid-ask spread): ~₹800
- Total monthly cost: ~₹2,300

That's 2.3% of your capital every month, just to run your strategy. Your gross monthly return needs to be 2.3% just to break even, before you pay any tax on profits. If you make 1.8% gross, you're losing 0.5% per month. Over a year, that's a 6% loss, not a 24% gain.

This is what he called the "new regime." It's not that the strategy failed. It's that the transaction costs rose faster than the strategy's alpha. And if you're a high-volume trader—say, turning over 100 times your capital per month—the costs are not a drag. They *are* the outcome. You are working full-time to pay the government, the exchange, and your broker.

"SETH" — as I call the interplay of STT, Exchange charges, Transaction charges, and His-own-fear — became a real acronym. As in: "I ran my backtest and my returns got seth-ed."

## Jane Street and the New Market Microstructure

Now we arrive at the title of this chapter, and the subject I've been circling.

If you looked at the average daily turnover in Indian equity derivatives during 2025–2026, you'd notice something odd. The total notional value was enormous—often crossing ₹150 lakh crore per month. But the *variety* of positions was shrinking. The vast majority of the volume was concentrated in a small number of indices: Nifty 50, Bank Nifty, and a handful of sector indices.

This concentration was not a coincidence. It was the fingerprint of systematic market-making. Firms like Jane Street (the New York-based proprietary trading house that had been expanding in India for years) operate on a simple principle: they don't take directional views. They provide liquidity on both sides and profit from the bid-ask spread. To do this profitably, they need:

1. **Deep, liquid, predictable underlyings** — hence the focus on indices.
2. **Ultra-low latency** — they're not thinking; they're responding.
3. **The ability to hedge instantaneously** — if they sell a Nifty call, they buy Nifty futures or Nifty Bank futures to stay delta-neutral.

I remember an email from a broker friend in Mumbai, sent in June 2025. He'd been asked to provide a client with something called a "combo quote"—a simultaneous bid and ask for a complex spread involving Nifty, Bank Nifty, and USD-INR options. The client was a regional bank looking to hedge a large FPI flow.

My friend laughed. "The pricing we got from Jane Street was 50 paise better than our own model. They're not just faster. They're *better priced*. I can't compete with people who can quote a two-way price in forty microseconds."

This is what he meant by "the new regime."

The entry of sophisticated algorithmic players has two consequences for ordinary investors. The first is *premium compression*. If you sell an option, you want a high premium. But when Jane Street is quoting 5 paise less than the theoretical fair value on the buy side and 5 paise more on the sell side, they are effectively squeezing the spread that a human market-maker would have pocketed. For non-professional option sellers, that means: your margin of error just shrank.

The second consequence is *wrong-way volatility*. In the old days, when Nifty spiked, option prices would gap because traders would panic. Now, when Nifty spikes, the algos instantly adjust their delta hedging. They don't panic—they buy or sell futures in a predictable, mechanical way. This *reduces* sharp spikes but *increases* the probability of small, repeated, unpredictable chop.

Let me show you what I mean with a comparison of two periods.

**Period A (2019–2021)**: Nifty's average daily range was 0.8%. When a major event occurred (e.g., a Covid cliff or a US election), the range would expand to 2–3%, and option implied volatility would spike. A trader could sell options before the event and buy them back after the IV crush, harvesting the "volatility risk premium."

**Period B (2025–2026)**: Nifty's average daily range was 0.5%. Events still moved it—tariff announcements, RBI policy, US data—but the range expansion was muted, and the post-event IV crush was smaller. Your option-selling strategy now earns less premium per unit of risk, and the tail risk (a sudden, sharp move) hasn't disappeared. It's just hidden in the shallow water.

If you're a retail trader who reads charts and follows news, this environment is exhausting. You see a breakout, you chase it, the breakthrough gets faded by algos within 90 seconds, you're stopped out. You see a breakdown, you sell, and the same algos buy the dip. Over time, you learn a perverse lesson: the only winning trade is *not trading*.

Which, of course, is exactly what the market makers want. Because if you stop trading, you stop taking the other side of their spread. And they make money whether the market goes up or down.

## The Rupee and the 25,000 Barrier

Let me return to the numbers.

From late 2024 through early 2026, the Nifty fluctuated in a band between roughly 23,000 and 26,000. On the upside, the profit of 26,000 was like the high water mark of a slow tide—touched, celebrated, then gone. On the downside, the floor of 24,000 held multiple times. A technician would have told you this was a textbook accumulation range: higher lows, lower highs, a coiled spring. But technicians are like astrologers—the few who were right in 2024 were wrong in 2025, and the ones who were wrong in 2025 were convinced they'd be right in 2026.

One structural factor kept the cap on the market: the rupee.

The rupee's slide from about 82 per dollar in late 2023 to over 91 by January 2026 was not a sudden collapse. It was a slow bleed. The Reserve Bank of India spent dollars defending the currency, but the structural pressure—widening current account deficit, FII outflows, and a strong dollar—was relentless. Each time the Nifty rose, foreign investors could sell the stocks and buy dollars at an increasingly favorable rate. This created a self-reinforcing loop: the flat index in rupee terms was a *falling* index in dollar terms. And that, in turn, justified further selling.

Let me use the worked example again.

Assume you're a global fund manager in New York. In September 2024, you allocate $100 million to India. You buy Nifty at 25,000. By December 2025, the Nifty is also at 25,000—the market has gone nowhere. But the rupee has moved from 83.5 to 91.2 to the dollar. When you convert your Indian holdings back to dollars, you get: $100 million × (83.5/91.2) = $91.55 million.

You didn't lose money in India. You lost money in *exchange rate*. And you'll think twice before reallocating to India next year.
