# Chapter 23: 2021–2022 – Liquidity Boom and Inflation Bust

The Zomato IPO listed on July 23, 2021. At 10:00 AM, the stock opened at ₹116, a 53% premium over its issue price of ₹76. On Zerodha and Groww, thousands of first-time investors watched their screens turn green. In the first hour, over 30 crore shares changed hands. By lunch, someone had made ₹2 lakh on an application of ₹15,200. By close, the stock had given back half its gains. Nobody cared about the afternoon slide. The message was clear: IPOs were a lottery, and everyone had a ticket.

This chapter is about what happens when easy money meets inexperience, and why the year that began with euphoria ended with a whiplash that separated durable wealth from paper gains.

## The Euphoria Machine

To understand 2021, you must first understand what happened to Indian savers during the pandemic.

Between March 2020 and December 2021, over 40 million new demat accounts were opened. That is roughly the population of Spain deciding to trade stocks. Most of these accounts belonged to people under 35, many opening their first investment account while sitting at home, staring at screens, with stimulus savings in their bank accounts and nowhere to spend them.

The mechanics were simple. Interest rates on fixed deposits had fallen to 3–4%. Real estate was frozen. Gold was expensive. The stock market, which had recovered from its March 2020 lows with surprising speed, looked like the only game in town. And the game had new rules: zero brokerage, instant account opening, and YouTube videos that made options trading look like a video game.

I recall a conversation in August 2021 with a young engineer from Pune. He had started trading in April 2020 with ₹50,000. By August 2021, his portfolio was worth ₹4.2 lakh. He had never read a balance sheet. He did not know what a P/E ratio meant. But he had bought Tata Motors at ₹120, sold at ₹480. He had bought IRCTC at ₹1,200, sold at ₹2,800. He was convinced he had found a system.

"Every time Nifty falls 2%, I buy more," he told me. "It always comes back."

This was the defining belief of 2021: that markets only go up. And for a while, the data supported it. Nifty rose from 14,000 in January 2021 to 18,000 by October. Every dip was bought within days. Mutual fund SIPs crossed ₹10,000 crore per month for the first time. Domestic institutional investors were buying everything in sight.

But here is what most retail investors missed: while they were buying, Foreign Institutional Investors were selling.

Between January and December 2021, FIIs sold ₹1.2 lakh crore of Indian equities. The market kept rising because domestic flows absorbed every bit of that selling. Retail investors were buying at prices that informed global money was exiting. The math was simple: if you are buying what smart money is selling, you better know something they do not. Most did not.

## The IPO Machine

The IPO market in 2021 was unlike anything India had seen. Between July and November, 47 companies raised over ₹1.1 lakh crore. Every IPO was oversubscribed. Some by 100 times, some by 200 times. And the listings—oh, the listings.

Zomato listed at a 53% premium. The stock was a restaurant aggregator that had never made a profit. But it had a brand name, a story about India's food delivery revolution, and a share price low enough that anyone with ₹15,000 could buy 100 shares. The IPO was oversubscribed 38 times. On listing day, the market valued Zomato at ₹1.2 lakh crore—more than the combined market cap of every restaurant chain operating in India.

Then came Paytm. The parent company, One97 Communications, had lost ₹2,400 crore in the previous financial year. Its IPO was the largest in Indian history: ₹18,300 crore. The valuation was ₹1.4 lakh crore. The issue was oversubscribed 1.9 times—weak by 2021 standards, but still enough to get the shares listed.

Paytm listed on November 18, 2021, at ₹1,950, a 9% discount to its issue price of ₹2,150. By close, it had fallen 27%. Investors who had applied hoping for a listing pop lost over ₹5,000 per share. On the first day, the company lost nearly ₹40,000 crore in market value.

I remember reading the news that evening. A man in Gujarat had borrowed ₹30 lakh from a private lender to apply for Paytm shares. He had expected to sell on listing day and repay the loan with profit. Instead, he was left with shares worth ₹22 lakh and a loan he could not repay.

This is a critical lesson, and I will state it plainly: **an IPO is not a gift. It is a transfer of ownership from early investors to the public. The early investors set the price. If they think ₹2,150 is a good price to sell, ask yourself why they would sell you something that is about to go up.**

## The Scorpion Sting

By February 2022, the euphoria was showing cracks. The US Federal Reserve had started talking about interest rate hikes. Inflation in America was running at 7.5%. Global bond yields were rising. And then, on February 24, Russia invaded Ukraine.

The market reaction was brutal. Nifty fell 10% in one week. Crude oil, which India imports 85% of, shot past $130 per barrel. The rupee weakened. FIIs, who had been selling for months, accelerated their exit. In March 2022 alone, they pulled out ₹62,000 crore.

The narrative shifted overnight. The same investors who had been buying every dip now panicked. The engineer from Pune—the one who had turned ₹50,000 into ₹4.2 lakh—saw his portfolio drop to ₹2.8 lakh in three weeks. He sold everything.

"It was too much," he told me later. "I couldn't sleep. I had never seen a real crash."

He had made money in a bull market and lost it in a correction. He had never learned to distinguish between skill and luck.

Here is the mathematics of the situation. In the first quarter of 2022, Nifty fell from 18,100 to 15,200—a decline of about 16%. The Nasdaq, by comparison, fell 30%. India fell less. Why? Because domestic flows continued. SIPs kept coming. Pension funds kept buying. The State Bank of India's mutual fund did not panic. HDFC Life did not sell. Indian households, directly or through mutual funds, absorbed the FII selling.

This is a structural fact that most analysts still misunderstand. When FIIs are selling and domestic institutions are buying, the headline numbers about FII outflow overstate the crash risk. Yes, ₹2.1 lakh crore of FII money left India in 2022. But domestic flows of ₹1.8 lakh crore absorbed most of it. The market fell, but not as much as it would have without that domestic cushion.

## When Correlations Fail

One of the most instructive events of this period was the chaos in bond markets. For decades, investors had relied on a simple relationship: when stocks fall, bonds rise. Gold, too, was supposed to act as a hedge. In 2022, every single one of these relationships broke.

Stocks fell. Bonds fell. Gold barely moved. The rupee fell against the dollar. Everything that was supposed to protect you during a crisis failed at the same time.

Why? Because the crisis was not a financial crisis. It was an inflation crisis. Central banks around the world were raising interest rates to fight inflation. When interest rates rise, both stocks and bonds lose value. Stocks fall because higher rates mean lower future profits. Bonds fall because existing bonds with lower interest rates become less attractive.

Gold, which had been a reliable safe haven for centuries, failed because the dollar was strengthening. Gold is priced in dollars. When the dollar rises, gold becomes more expensive for non-dollar buyers, so demand falls.

I remember looking at a portfolio in September 2022. An investor had built a "diversified" portfolio in November 2021: 60% equity, 30% debt, 10% gold. On paper, it looked balanced. In reality, after one year of the war and rate hikes, the equity was down 8%, the debt was down 5%, and the gold was flat. The portfolio had returned about 2%—barely beating inflation. The diversification had not protected capital. It had only slowed the bleeding.

This is the lesson of 2022: **diversification works over decades, not years. In a synchronized global shock, everything that can correlate will correlate.**

## A Worked Example: The ₹7,800 Portfolio

To make this concrete, let me walk you through a real portfolio I reviewed in November 2022. An investor had built a small portfolio—about ₹7,800—at Diwali 2021, when Nifty was near 18,000. The allocation was:

- 40% in a Nifty index ETF
- 20% in a mid-cap mutual fund
- 15% in a liquid fund (debt)
- 10% in a gold ETF
- 10% in a REIT (Real Estate Investment Trust)
- 5% in a single stock: Power Grid Corporation

One year later, in November 2022, Nifty was at 18,300—roughly flat. But the portfolio was not flat. Here is what happened:

The Nifty index ETF returned about 2% (price return plus dividends). The mid-cap fund fell 6%. The liquid fund returned 4.5% (interest, after expenses). The gold ETF returned 1%. The REIT returned 3% (rental yield plus slight price appreciation).

And Power Grid? It returned 28%—14% price appreciation plus ₹13 per share in dividends. The single stock, which was only 5% of the portfolio, contributed more to total return than the index ETF.

Total portfolio return: approximately 2-3%. Not bad for a year when stocks and bonds fell together. Not good compared to inflation, which was running at 6-7%.

The lesson? The portfolio worked—barely—because it had multiple asset classes. But the real story was that the single stock, chosen not for any technical reason but because the investor worked in the power sector and understood the business, had saved the portfolio from a negative year.

## The Adani Mirage

No discussion of 2021–2022 is complete without mentioning the Adani group.

Between January 2020 and September 2022, Adani Group stocks gained between 4x and 8x. Adani Enterprises went from ₹100 to ₹3,300. Adani Green Energy went from ₹150 to ₹2,200. Adani Total Gas went from ₹80 to ₹3,800. The combined market cap of Adani Group companies crossed ₹19 lakh crore—larger than the entire stock market of South Korea.

Adani Ports, the most tangible business in the group, was valued at 30 times earnings. Adani Green, which had never paid a dividend, was valued at 400 times earnings. Adani Total Gas, which delivered gas to households on a regulated margin, was valued at 500 times earnings.

The bull case was that the Adani group controlled critical infrastructure—ports, airports, power plants, transmission lines—that would benefit from India's growth story. The bear case was that the valuations implied growth that had never been achieved by any Indian conglomerate in history.

I will not predict crashes. I will only say this: when a stock goes up 8x in two years while its business grows 2x, the 4x gap is not value creation. It is sentiment. And sentiment can reverse faster than it formed.

## The Lesson of the Whipsaw

By December 2022, Nifty was back at 18,100—roughly where it had started in October 2021. Two years of drama, war, inflation, and record FII selling had produced exactly zero return for the index.

But beneath the index, the damage was uneven. Stocks that had doubled in 2021 were down 40-50% in 2022. IPOs that had listed at 50% premiums were trading below their issue prices. And the retail investors who had entered in 2021, buying every dip and celebrating every listing, had learned a painful lesson: markets are not ATMs.

The ones who survived were not the ones who timed the crash. They were the ones who had bought businesses they understood, at prices that made sense, with money they did not need for five years.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Euphoria has markers:** Record IPO supply, instant doubling on listing, and the belief that every dip is a buying opportunity are signs that the easy money has been made. When 10 IPOs list in a week and all of them double, the next 10 will not.

2. **Domestic flows matter:** India's market structure has changed. ₹1.2 lakh crore of annual SIP flows and pension fund buying create a floor that did not exist in 2008 or 2013. FII selling is painful but not fatal.

3. **Correlation fails in inflation crises:** The traditional portfolio of 60% equity and 40% debt works in recessions. In inflation crises, both fall. Diversification across genuinely different assets—including cash, commodities, and international equity—is the only defense.

4. **Valuation is the only thing that matters in the end:** A stock that goes up 8x while its business grows 2x has a 4x gap that must be closed. It can close through price falls, or through years of business growth. Either way, the gap exists.

5. **Saving is not the same as investing:** Opening a demat account and buying shares is saving. Understanding what you own, at what price, and for how long—that is investing. The two are not the same, and the market will eventually teach you the difference.
