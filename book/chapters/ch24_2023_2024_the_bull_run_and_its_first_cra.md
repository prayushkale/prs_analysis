# Chapter 24: 2023-2024 – The Bull Run and Its First Crack

## A Market Chronicle 2018-2026

---

The Nifty opened at 18,197 on January 2, 2023. By December 29, it would close at 21,731. A gain of 19.4% in a single calendar year—respectable, but not remarkable by Indian standards. What made this year different was what happened beneath the surface: the machinery of the market had changed, and most participants didn't notice until it was too late.

Let me take you back to a specific Tuesday in March 2023. I was sitting in my study, watching the HDFC Bank stock chart on a secondary monitor. The merger with HDFC Ltd had been announced in April 2022, but the actual effective date—July 1, 2023—was still months away. Yet the stock was already behaving strangely. It had fallen from ₹1,700 in December 2022 to ₹1,550 in March 2023. Nothing in the fundamentals explained it. The bank was reporting 18% loan growth, stable NIMs, and falling NPAs. The selloff was mechanical.

This is where most retail investors make their first mistake. They see a price drop and assume something is wrong. They sell, or they avoid buying. What they don't see is the plumbing.

**The Merger Mechanics That Moved Markets**

The HDFC-HDFC Bank merger was not just a corporate event—it was the largest forced portfolio rebalancing in Indian market history. Here's why it mattered to every single person holding a Nifty index fund or ETF.

Before the merger, HDFC Ltd was a separate company with a 7.8% weight in the Nifty 50. HDFC Bank had a 5.2% weight. Combined, they represented 13% of the index. After the merger, HDFC Ltd would cease to exist, and HDFC Bank would absorb its weight. But here's the catch: index rules cap any single stock at roughly 10-12% of the Nifty. So HDFC Bank couldn't simply inherit the full 13%. The excess weight—about 3-4%—had to be redistributed to the other 49 stocks.

Now think about what this means in practice. Every index fund tracking the Nifty 50—and there were hundreds of crores of rupees in such funds—had to sell HDFC Ltd shares and buy HDFC Bank shares. But they couldn't do it all at once. The merger had a specific timeline: HDFC Ltd shares would be delisted, and HDFC Bank shares would be issued to former HDFC Ltd shareholders. During this transition, index funds had to hold cash or equivalents, then deploy it into HDFC Bank shares at the new weight.

The result was a multi-month flow pattern that professional traders could anticipate. From March to June 2023, HDFC Bank was under constant selling pressure from index funds that were reducing their exposure to the combined entity. The stock fell from ₹1,550 to ₹1,450. Retail investors panicked. Smart money accumulated.

Let me give you a worked example. Suppose you had ₹10 lakh in a Nifty index fund on January 1, 2023. Your fund held roughly ₹78,000 in HDFC Ltd and ₹52,000 in HDFC Bank. By July 1, after the merger, your fund would hold roughly ₹1.3 lakh in HDFC Bank—but only if the fund manager executed the transition correctly. If he sold HDFC Ltd too early and bought HDFC Bank too late, you'd lose money to the spread. If he timed it right, you'd capture the arbitrage.

This is not insider trading. This is understanding the rules of the game. The index rebalancing calendar is public. The weight transfer mechanics are published by NSE. Anyone who took the time to study them could have predicted that HDFC Bank would face selling pressure from March to June, then buying pressure from July to September.

And that's exactly what happened. HDFC Bank bottomed at ₹1,420 in June 2023. By September, it was back at ₹1,650. The mechanical selling had reversed into mechanical buying.

**The Two-Year Flat Market That Fooled Everyone**

Here's a fact that will surprise you: from January 2022 to December 2023, the Nifty 50 returned approximately 0% in absolute terms. Two full years of zero returns. If you had bought a Nifty index fund on January 1, 2022, and sold it on December 31, 2023, you would have made nothing—before expenses and taxes, you'd have lost money.

But during those same two years, the Nasdaq 100 in the United States rose roughly 50%. NVIDIA alone doubled in three months on the AI frenzy. Indian retail investors looked at their portfolios, saw flat returns, and felt like they were missing out. They started chasing momentum—small caps, mid caps, thematic funds. The BSE Smallcap index rose 47% in 2023 alone. The BSE Midcap index rose 44%.

This is where the second mistake happens. When the headline index goes nowhere but individual stocks are flying, people assume the index is broken. They abandon it. They chase the hot sectors. They forget that two years of near-zero index returns historically precede above-FD multi-year returns in any rolling 3-5 year window.

I've seen this pattern three times in my career: 2003-2004, 2013-2014, and 2022-2023. Each time, the market went flat for two years, then exploded upward. Each time, investors who stayed disciplined in the index were rewarded. Each time, those who chased momentum got burned when the rotation came.

**The First Crack: June 4, 2024**

The bull run of 2023-2024 was built on three pillars: domestic liquidity, corporate earnings recovery, and political stability. The first two were solid. The third turned out to be an illusion.

On June 4, 2024, Lok Sabha election results were announced. The exit polls had predicted a landslide for the BJP-led NDA—400+ seats. The market had priced this in. Nifty was at 23,300 on June 3, just shy of all-time highs. Everyone expected a gap-up opening on June 4.

Instead, the Nifty opened at 22,500—down 3.4%. Within minutes, it was at 21,800. By 10:30 AM, it touched 21,281. An intraday fall of 8.5%. The Sensex fell 6,000 points in a single session. ₹30 lakh crore of market capitalization evaporated in four hours.

Why? The BJP-led NDA was short of a majority. They had won only 293 seats, not the 400+ that had been priced in. The market had priced in a supermajority. It got a coalition government.

Let me pause here and explain something important about how markets price political events. They don't price the outcome. They price the *surprise*. If everyone expects 400 seats and you get 293, that's a negative surprise of 107 seats. The market doesn't care that 293 is still a majority. It cares that the expectation was wrong.

This is why I've always told my students: "Markets move on surprise, not on news." The news was that the NDA would form the government. That was known. The surprise was that they needed coalition partners—TDP's Chandrababu Naidu and JDU's Nitish Kumar—to get there.

The recovery was equally instructive. By the end of that day, the Nifty had recovered to 22,000—still down 5.9%, but off the lows. Over the next three months, the index would scale record highs 32 times, reaching 26,000 by late September 2024. The June 4 crash became a footnote.

But here's what most people missed: the crack was not the crash itself. The crack was what it revealed about market structure.

**The Domestic Liquidity Shield**

During the June 4 crash, foreign institutional investors (FIIs) sold ₹5,800 crore. But domestic institutional investors (DIIs) bought ₹7,200 crore. Mutual funds, insurance companies, and pension funds absorbed the selling. The Nifty fell 6% instead of 15%.

This was not an accident. It was the result of a structural shift that had been building since 2020. Retail SIPs had grown from ₹8,000 crore per month in 2019 to ₹18,000 crore per month in 2024. EPFO and NPS contributions were flowing into equity markets automatically. The domestic liquidity machine had become large enough to absorb FII selling.

I remember a conversation I had in September 2022, when the Nifty had fallen from 18,000 to 15,200 while the Nasdaq fell 30%. Everyone was asking: "Why is India falling less?" The answer was simple: record retail participation. When FIIs sold ₹2.1 lakh crore in 2022, domestic investors bought ₹2.3 lakh crore. The net effect was zero.

This is a lesson that most market participants still don't understand. When you see headlines about "FIIs selling ₹10,000 crore," don't panic. Ask the follow-up question: "How much are DIIs buying?" If the answer is ₹12,000 crore, the selling is being absorbed. The headline is noise.

**The Valuation Trap**

By December 2023, the Nifty was trading at a price-to-earnings ratio of roughly 30-35. Other emerging markets were trading at 10-12. India's valuation premium—about 3x—was at an all-time high.

This is where the third mistake happens. Investors see high valuations and assume a crash is coming. They sell their positions and wait for a better entry. But markets can stay expensive for years. The Nifty traded at 25+ PE from 2021 to 2024 without a major correction. Investors who sold in 2021 because "valuations are too high" missed a 40% rally.

The correct framework is not "Is the market expensive?" but "Is the premium justified?" India was growing at 6-7% GDP while other EMs were growing at 3-4%. Indian corporate earnings were growing at 15-18% while global earnings were flat. The premium was not irrational—it was a reflection of differential growth.

But here's the warning I gave in my December 2023 newsletter: "Asset classes wildly outperform for a stretch, then go lacklustre for years. Never extrapolate a hot phase." The Nifty had returned 19% in 2023. It would return 12% in 2024. Not bad, but not the 30% that momentum chasers expected.

**The 24,000 Target and How It Was Derived**

In early 2024, I published a target of 24,000 for the Nifty by year-end. This was not a guess. It was derived from four independent arguments:

1. **VIX-implied move:** The India VIX was trading at 12-14, implying a 9-10% annualized move. From the January level of 21,700, that gave a range of 19,500 to 24,000.

2. **Risk premium over FDs:** Fixed deposits were offering 7-8%. The equity risk premium—the extra return investors demand for taking stock market risk—was about 4-5%. That implied a fair return of 11-13% for the Nifty, which gave 24,000-24,500.

3. **Rollover hurdle:** The Nifty rolls forward at roughly 170 points per month. By December 2024, the rollover would add about 2,000 points to the index level. From 21,700, that gave 23,700.

4. **Mean reversion:** Two years of zero returns (2022-2023) historically preceded above-FD returns. If the Nifty returned 15% in 2024, that gave 24,900.

The average of these four methods was 24,150. I rounded to 24,000. The Nifty hit 24,000 in July 2024 and peaked at 26,277 in September.

This is how professional targets are derived—not from vibes or gut feelings, but from measurable anchors. You can disagree with the assumptions, but you can't argue with the method.

**The Lesson of 2023-2024**

The bull run of 2023-2024 was not a straight line. It was a series of cracks and repairs. The HDFC merger created a mechanical buying opportunity. The two-year flat market created a mean-reversion opportunity. The June 4 crash created a buying opportunity for those who understood that domestic liquidity would absorb the selling.

But the biggest lesson was this: markets are not rational in the short term. They are mechanical. They follow rules. They overshoot on both sides. The investor who understands the plumbing—the index rebalancing, the flow mechanics, the valuation frameworks—will always have an edge over the investor who only watches the news.

In the next chapter, we'll see how the 2024-2025 period tested this framework with a new set of challenges: the return of FII selling, the small-cap bubble, and the first signs of a global slowdown.

---

**Key Takeaways**

1. **Index rebalancing creates predictable flow patterns.** The HDFC-HDFC Bank merger caused mechanical selling from March to June 2023, then mechanical buying from July to September. Study the index calendar—it's public information.

2. **Two years of flat index returns historically precede multi-year bull runs.** The Nifty returned ~0% from 2022-2023, then rallied 40%+ in 2024. Patience in the index beats chasing momentum.

3. **Markets move on surprise, not news.** The June 4 crash was not about the NDA winning—it was about the NDA winning *less than expected*. Always ask: "What is the market pricing in, and what is the surprise?"

4. **Domestic liquidity is now large enough to absorb FII selling.** When you see "FIIs sold ₹10,000 crore," check DII buying. If DIIs are buying ₹12,000 crore, the net effect is positive. Don't panic over headline numbers.

5. **Derive targets from measurable anchors, not vibes.** Use VIX-implied moves, risk premium over FDs, rollover hurdles, and mean reversion. Average them. You'll get a range, not a guess.
