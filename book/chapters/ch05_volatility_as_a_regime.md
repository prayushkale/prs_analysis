# Chapter 5: Volatility as a Regime

## How This Market Works

The market opened and HDFC Bank dropped like a stone. Within minutes, it was down 5%. By noon, it had lost 8.5%. In a single session, India's most valuable private sector bank shed over ₹1 lakh crore in market capitalization. The index lurched. Traders who had bought the "safe" stock for its stability were staring at margin calls. That day taught us something important: volatility is not an exception. It is the default state of this market. What we call "normal" is just a pause between violent movements.

## The Nature of Market Volatility

Most retail traders begin their journey believing the market is a place of orderly progress. Stocks go up slowly over time. Corrections are rare. Crashes are once-in-a-decade events. This belief is the single most expensive myth you will ever encounter.

Let me show you what the data actually says.

In 2024 alone, the Nifty 50 hit record highs 32 times. That sounds like a smooth upward march until you look closer. The same year, on June 4, the index fell 8.5% intraday—one of the largest single-day drops in a decade. From that low, it recovered and climbed to new highs, but the path was never straight.

Consider this: between January 2020 and December 2024, the Nifty experienced:

- 13 moves of 5% or more in a single week
- 4 corrections of 10% or more
- 2 instances of circuit breakers halting trading

This is not a calm market. This is a market that moves in violent, unpredictable bursts, followed by periods of deceptive stillness.

## The Volatility Regime Framework

Before I explain how to trade volatility, you need to understand what it actually is. Most people think volatility means "risk." That is imprecise.

Volatility is simply the rate of change in price. A stock that moves 1% every day has a certain level of volatility. A stock that moves 5% every day has higher volatility. Neither is good or bad by itself—they are just different regimes.

Think of it like weather. A city that gets 200 days of rain per year is not "bad" compared to a city that gets 50 days of rain. They are simply different climates. The mistake is to use a desert strategy in a rainforest.

In markets, we have three primary volatility regimes:

**Low Volatility Regime:**
- Daily moves under 0.5%
- India VIX below 12
- Options premiums are cheap
- This is when most retail traders get comfortable

**Normal Volatility Regime:**
- Daily moves between 0.5% and 1.5%
- India VIX between 12 and 18
- Options premiums are moderately priced
- Most strategies work reasonably well

**High Volatility Regime:**
- Daily moves exceeding 1.5%
- India VIX above 18
- Options premiums are expensive
- Most standard strategies fail

The critical insight is this: **markets can switch regimes faster than most traders can adapt.**

## The Anatomy of a Volatility Event

Let me walk you through the June 4, 2024 event in detail. This is not a history lesson—it is a case study in how volatility regimes function.

**The Setup:**
The market had been rallying for months. Nifty was near 23,500. The India VIX, a measure of expected volatility, had been hovering around 13—relatively calm. Most traders were positioned for a "normal" election result. Options premiums were low. Call writing was popular.

**The Trigger:**
As vote counting progressed through the morning, it became clear that the BJP-led NDA would not achieve the supermajority that markets had priced in. The gap between expectations and reality created a shock.

**The Cascade:**
At 9:15 AM, Nifty was at 23,400. By 10:30 AM, it had fallen to 22,800. By 12:30 PM, it touched 21,281—a drop of over 8.5% from the previous close.

But here is what most people miss: **the actual damage was far worse than the index numbers suggest.**

Individual stocks fell 15-20%. Small-cap stocks dropped 25-30%. The volatility index, India VIX, spiked from 13 to over 30 in a matter of hours. Options that were worth ₹10 in the morning became worth ₹200 by afternoon. Traders who had sold options for modest premiums faced catastrophic losses.

The market had entered a high volatility regime.

## The Mechanics of Regime Change

How does a low-volatility regime turn into a high-volatility regime? It happens through three mechanisms:

**1. Positioning Liquidation:**
When a large number of traders are on the same side of the trade, and the market moves against them, they must close positions. This forced selling creates additional momentum. In June 2024, FIIs had been heavy buyers of index futures. When the market dropped, they had to sell—which pushed prices lower, triggering more selling.

**2. Margin Cascades:**
In low volatility, traders lever up. They buy more with less margin. When volatility spikes, margin requirements increase. A trader who was using 4x leverage with a ₹1 lakh position now needs to put up 2x more capital. If they don't have it, their broker sells their positions automatically. One forced sale becomes ten becomes a hundred.

**3. Option Gamma Effects:**
This is the most misunderstood mechanism. When options are cheap (low volatility), many traders sell them. These option sellers are "short gamma"—they are forced to buy when the market falls and sell when it rises, amplifying the moves.

On June 4, the option sellers who had been collecting easy premiums for months had to buy enormous amounts of stock and index futures to hedge their suddenly deep-in-the-money put options. This buying did not stop the fall—it just made the volatility worse.

## The Dhanraj Playbook for Volatility Regimes

I have spent over a decade watching these regime changes. Here is what I have learned.

**First, do not forecast the direction. Forecast the regime.**

Most traders waste time trying to predict whether Nifty will go to 25,000 or 24,000. This is a fool's errand. What matters is whether we are in a regime that will see 1% daily moves or 0.3% daily moves.

In January 2024, when HDFC Bank dropped 8.5%, the stock had been moving in a 2% range for weeks. The volatility regime changed without warning. Anyone who was positioned for low volatility lost money. Anyone who was prepared for high volatility had a chance to profit.

**Second, size for the regime, not the trade.**

When volatility is low, you can afford to take larger positions. When volatility is high, you must reduce your position size proportionally.

Here is a simple rule I use:

- If India VIX is below 12, I can take positions that use 100% of my normal sizing
- If India VIX is between 12 and 20, I reduce to 75% of normal sizing
- If India VIX is above 20, I reduce to 50% or less

This is not about predicting the market. It is about surviving it.

**Third, use the volatility itself as a signal.**

The market tells you what regime you are in through price action. You just have to listen.

When you see:
- Consecutive days of 1%+ moves in either direction
- Options premiums expanding rapidly
- Margin calls becoming common in trader circles

These are signs that the regime has shifted. Do not fight it. Adjust your approach.

## A Worked Example: Trading HDFC Bank in January 2024

Let me illustrate how a regime-aware trader would have navigated the HDFC Bank crash.

**Before the Event (Low Volatility Regime):**
HDFC Bank was trading around ₹1,700. It had been moving in a ₹30-40 range daily. Option premiums were cheap. A trader selling a ₹1,700 put for a ₹15 premium seemed like easy money.

**The Regime Change:**
On January 16, the stock opened at ₹1,640 and dropped to ₹1,550 within hours. The daily range expanded from ₹35 to ₹150. The option premium on that ₹1,700 put went from ₹15 to ₹180 overnight.

**The Regime-Aware Response:**
A trader who recognized the regime change would have:
1. Closed all short option positions immediately
2. Bought out-of-the-money puts as a hedge (costing maybe ₹10-15 per lot)
3. Waited for the volatility spike to subside before taking any new positions

The trader who ignored the regime change and held onto the short put would have lost ₹1.65 lakh on a position that originally collected only ₹15,000 in premium.

**The Recovery:**
After the initial crash, HDFC Bank stabilized around ₹1,500. The volatility began to decline. A regime-aware trader would then look for opportunities to sell options again—but only after the India VIX (or stock-specific implied volatility) had come down from its peak.

## The Tools for Reading Volatility Regimes

You do not need complex software to understand volatility regimes. You need three things:

**1. The India VIX Index:**
This is the market's estimate of 30-day volatility in Nifty. It is calculated from option prices. When it is low, options are cheap. When it is high, options are expensive.

**2. The Average True Range (ATR):**
This measures how much a stock or index actually moves each day. If the 14-day ATR of Nifty is 150 points and suddenly becomes 400 points, the regime has changed.

**3. The Put-Call Ratio:**
When this ratio spikes above 1.3 or drops below 0.7, it often signals an extreme in sentiment that can precede a regime change.

But here is the truth: none of these tools will tell you when the regime will change. They will only tell you that it has changed.

## The Trap of the "New Normal"

In October 2024, I watched traders who had survived the June crash make a different mistake. The market had been rallying for months. Volatility was low. India VIX was hovering around 13. These traders decided volatility was "dead" and doubled down on selling options.

Then came the October consolidation. Nifty moved in a 300-point range for weeks. The volatility was low, but it was deceptive—option sellers were collecting tiny premiums for positions that could still blow up if the regime changed.

The market does not owe you a repeat of the past. Every regime eventually ends. The skill is not in predicting when—it is in never being destroyed by the transition.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Volatility is not an exception; it is the default state.** Markets spend most of their time in volatile regimes. The periods of calm are the exceptions.

2. **Do not forecast direction; forecast the regime.** Your position sizing and strategy should change based on whether we are in a low, normal, or high volatility environment.

3. **When the regime changes, act immediately.** Closing positions at a loss is preferable to holding through a regime you are not prepared for.

4. **Use the India VIX, ATR, and put-call ratio as regime indicators**, but understand that they confirm the present—they do not predict the future.

5. **Sizing for the regime matters more than any individual trade.** A properly sized trade in a high-volatility regime will outperform an oversized trade in a calm market.

---

The market is not a machine that produces predictable outcomes. It is a living system that cycles through states of calm and chaos. Your job as a trader is not to fight these cycles. It is to recognize which cycle you are in and act accordingly.

Rahul, the young trader who paced my office during the HDFC crash, learned this lesson the hard way. He lost a year's worth of trading profits in a single day. But he also learned something more valuable: the market does not care about your plans. It cares about the regime you are in.

He trades differently now. He watches the volatility first, the price second. He is not the most profitable trader I know. But he is one of the few who has survived long enough to keep learning.
