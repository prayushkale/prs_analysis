# Chapter 30: The Hard Assets Debate

## Wealth Beyond the Screen

I remember a conversation in early 2022 with a retired government officer in Pune. He had saved diligently for thirty years and built a corpus of roughly ₹2.5 crore. Half of it sat in a fixed deposit yielding 5.5%. The other half was his ancestral home in a prime locality, valued at around ₹1.2 crore. He was proud of that house. "Land is the only thing they're not making more of," he told me, repeating a line I have heard from a hundred different investors across India.

He asked me what he should do. His son wanted to sell the house and invest the proceeds in a mix of equity mutual funds and bonds. The father resisted. "Stocks are paper. This house is real. You can touch it. You can live in it. No broker can steal it."

That is the hard assets debate in its purest form. The tug-of-war between what feels tangible and what actually generates wealth. In this chapter, I want to put both sides on the table, examine the numbers honestly, and help you decide where your own money belongs.

---

## What Makes an Asset "Hard"?

The phrase *hard asset* sounds reassuring. It suggests something solid, indestructible, proven across centuries. Gold, real estate, land, even commodities like crude oil or copper—these are the classic hard assets. They have intrinsic use. You can build a house on land. You can wear gold. You can burn oil.

Financial assets—stocks, bonds, mutual funds, ETFs—are called *paper assets* or *soft assets*. They exist only as entries in a database. A share of ITC is just an electronic record. You cannot live in it, wear it, or burn it for warmth.

That distinction feels important to most Indians. It is why the average household in India holds over 77% of its wealth in real estate, according to several industry surveys. Compare that to the United States, where real estate makes up roughly 25-30% of household net worth. The difference is not just about affordability or tax. It is about trust.

But trust and returns are not the same thing.

---

## The Real Estate Reality Check

Let me give you the numbers without softening them. In India, residential rental yields—the annual rent you receive as a percentage of the property's market value—hover between 2% and 3%. In Mumbai's prime areas, you are lucky to get 2.2%. In Delhi's nicer suburbs, perhaps 2.5%. In smaller cities, yields can touch 3.5% but rarely cross 4%.

Now compare that to the cost of borrowing. A home loan today costs around 8.5% to 9.5% per annum. Even if you own the property outright, the opportunity cost of that capital matters. If your ₹1 crore flat gives you ₹2.5 lakh in annual rent (2.5% yield), but you could have earned ₹8.5 lakh by putting that ₹1 crore in a safe debt fund (at 8.5% pre-tax), you are losing ₹6 lakh every year in potential income. That is the *negative carry* of Indian real estate.

I am not saying real estate cannot appreciate. Plenty of properties have doubled or tripled over ten-year periods. But here is the catch: price appreciation in real estate is lumpy, unpredictable, and increasingly concentrated in specific micro-markets. The national average over the last decade, as per the RBI's House Price Index, has been roughly 4-5% per annum in nominal terms. After adjusting for inflation of 4-6%, the real return is close to zero. And that is before you account for registration costs (5-6% in most states), brokerage, stamp duty, maintenance, property tax, and the occasional need to bribe a tenant to vacate.

**A worked example:**

Take a flat worth ₹1 crore bought in 2015 in a mid-sized city. Assume 4% annual appreciation—a reasonable assumption for a well-located property in a decent market. After ten years, in 2025, the property is worth roughly ₹1.48 crore. Assume you got 2.5% rental yield each year, which is ₹2.5 lakh annually, or ₹25 lakh over ten years. But assume maintenance, property tax, and miscellaneous costs eat up 30% of that rent, leaving you ₹17.5 lakh net rental income.

Your total wealth from the property: ₹1.48 crore (sale value) + ₹0.175 crore (net rent) = ₹1.655 crore.

But you paid 5% registration and stamp duty at purchase: ₹5 lakh. That cost should be added to your investment, reducing effective returns. And if you borrowed money, the interest cost would demolish these numbers entirely.

Now compare. Invest that same ₹1 crore in 2015 into a balanced portfolio of 60% Nifty 50 index and 40% Bharat Bond ETF (or a comparable debt fund). Let the Nifty grow at 12% CAGR (its long-term average is about 14-15% but let's be conservative) and the debt component at 7% CAGR. Rebalance yearly.

After ten years, the equity portion of ₹60 lakh grows to roughly ₹1.86 crore. The debt portion of ₹40 lakh grows to ₹78.7 lakh. Total: ₹2.647 crore. That is almost ₹1 crore more than the real estate investment. And you had no repair bills, no tenant headaches, no registration costs, and you could sell any portion of that portfolio in two business days if you needed cash.

The point is not that real estate is always bad. It is that most Indians do not do this math. They see their neighbour's flat double in value and assume that is the norm, ignoring the years of zero growth, the stamp duty pain, and the rent they could have earned elsewhere.

## The Case for One House

I want to be careful. I am not saying you should never own a home. There is a powerful emotional and social argument for owning the house you live in. In India, owning a home is tied to security, status, and often marriage eligibility. I have seen families where a ₹1 crore flat unlocked a match that would have been impossible otherwise. That is a real return, just not one you can capture in a spreadsheet.

Also, you cannot put a price on the freedom to paint your walls, keep pets, or not worry about a landlord raising rent. For these reasons, I believe you should own exactly one self-occupied home. Treat it as consumption, not investment. Do not expect it to fund your retirement. Buy it because it makes your life better, not because you think it will outperform stocks.

But the moment you buy a second property, you are making an investment decision. And that decision needs to be held to the same standards as any other investment. If the rental yield is 2.5% and your loan interest is 8.5%, you are bleeding money every month. The only way you make a profit is if prices rise faster than your carrying cost. That is gambling on appreciation, not investing.

## Gold: The Ancient Hard Asset

Gold is the other pillar of Indian hard assets. Almost every family has some—wedding jewellery, coins, bars. It is liquid in small quantities, trusted across generations, and holds value through political chaos.

The historical long-term return of gold in India, measured in rupees, is about 8-9% CAGR over the past 20 years. But there are three problems.

First, gold produces no income. No dividend, no rent, no interest. You only make money when you sell it to someone else at a higher price. That makes it a speculative asset over the medium term.

Second, storage and safety are real costs. Jewellery has making charges (6-20%) that are lost when you sell. Gold ETFs and sovereign gold bonds solve this, but then you are back to paper assets.

Third, gold's returns have been volatile. From 2011 to 2015, gold fell roughly 30% in rupee terms while the Nifty doubled. From 2020 to 2024, gold did well again, but it lagged equities.

I personally keep 5-10% of my portfolio in gold (via sovereign gold bonds for the extra 2.5% interest) as a hedge against extreme events. But I do not expect it to build wealth. It is insurance, not an engine of growth.

## The REIT Alternative

If you love real estate but want liquidity, look at Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs). In India, listed REITs like Embassy Office Parks, Mindspace, and Brookfield India own commercial office buildings and pay out 90% of their rental income as dividends. Current yields are around 6-7%—nearly triple what you get from a residential flat.

And REITs are traded on the stock exchange. You can buy or sell ₹10 lakh worth of units in minutes. No registration fees, no property tax hassles, no dealing with tenants who stop paying rent.

The obvious objection is that REITs are paper. You do not own a physical building; you own a unit in a trust. But the underlying asset is still real estate. The trust holds the title deed. You have a legal claim on the rental income. And because it is regulated by SEBI, the governance is far better than a typical landlord-tenant arrangement.

For anyone who wants real estate exposure without the headaches, REITs are the smarter choice. The numbers simply work better.

## The Structural Trap of Hard Assets in India

There is a deeper reason hard assets underperform in India: the tax system.

When you buy a second property, you pay stamp duty (5-6%). When you sell it, you pay capital gains tax (20% with indexation if held over three years). The rental income is taxed at your slab rate. So the government takes a cut at every step.

Meanwhile, equity mutual funds held for more than one year attract 10% tax on gains above ₹1 lakh. Debt funds held for more than three years are taxed at 20% with indexation, which often brings the effective tax rate close to zero for long holding periods.

And then there is the Bharat Bond ETF example I often cite. A 10-year Bharat Bond currently yields about 7.2% pre-tax. If you hold it for three years, you qualify for indexation. For an investor in the 30% tax bracket, the post-tax return can exceed 6.5%. A fixed deposit of the same tenure offers 6% pre-tax but gets taxed annually at your slab rate, leaving you with about 4.2% post-tax. That difference of over 2 percentage points compounded over a decade becomes enormous.

Why does this matter? Because hard asset enthusiasts often ignore the tax drag. They see the gross rental yield of 2.5% and think, "At least it's something." But after property tax, maintenance, and income tax on rent, the net yield is closer to 1.5%. That is worse than keeping money in a savings account.

Financial assets, when structured properly, defer taxes (through indexation) or reduce them (through lower rates on long-term capital gains). Hard assets do not offer that benefit.

## When Hard Assets Win

I would be dishonest if I told you hard assets never outperform. They do, in specific windows.

- If you bought land on the outskirts of a city before a highway or metro line was announced, you could have multiplied your money many times over.
- If you bought gold in 2002 at ₹5,000 per 10 grams and sold in 2024 at ₹72,000, you made 14% CAGR—beating the Nifty over that same period (roughly 16% CAGR) by a small margin considering risk.
- If you bought a shop in a commercial complex in a rapidly growing tier-2 city, you might have achieved rental yields of 6-7% during the boom years.

But these are exceptions, not rules. They require timing, local knowledge, and often luck. The average investor buying a flat in a metro city through a broker—the most common real estate trade in India—has not done well over the last decade after all costs.

My advice to that retired officer in Pune was simple. Keep the ancestral home if your son is willing to live in it or if it gives you emotional peace. But if you are holding it purely as an investment, consider selling, paying the capital gains tax, and moving the money into a combination of equity index funds, Bharat Bond ETFs, and a REIT. Over the next twenty years, that portfolio will almost certainly give you a better retirement than a house that leaks, demands repairs, and pays you 2% rent.

He did not sell. And I understand why. Hard assets have a psychological pull that spreadsheets cannot capture. But if you are reading this book, you are trying to build wealth systematically. So let the numbers guide you, not the feeling of touching a wall.

---

## Key Takeaways

1. **Indian residential real estate is a poor investment for most people.** Rental yields of 2-3% cannot compete with loan rates of 8-9% or with the long-term returns of a balanced financial portfolio.

2. **Own one home for lifestyle, not for returns.** Treat it as consumption. If you own more than one, treat the additional properties with the same rigor as any other investment.

3. **Gold is insurance, not wealth creation.** Keep 5-10% of your portfolio in gold for crisis protection, but do not expect it to fund your retirement.

4. **For real estate exposure, consider REITs.** They offer better yields (6-7%), full liquidity, and no maintenance hassles.

5. **Tax efficiency favours financial assets.** Indexation on debt funds and lower capital gains tax on equities make paper assets more attractive than hard assets over the long term. Always calculate post-tax, post-cost returns before deciding.
