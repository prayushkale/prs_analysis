# Chapter 1: Why Most Traders Lose

## The Trader's Mind

Let me take you back to the morning of June 4, 2024.

The Nifty opened at 23,339. Nothing unusual—the index had been grinding higher for months, touching new all-time highs with boring regularity. Traders sat at their desks, F&O expiry still three days away, expecting a routine session of range-bound noise.

Then the first election trends hit the screens.

By 9:30 AM, the Nifty had lost 500 points. By 10:15, it was down 1,000. By 11:30, the index was plunging through 22,000. Panic—real, visceral, contagious panic—had taken over the market. The Nifty would eventually touch 21,281, a fall of over 8.5% from the previous close. Circuit breakers kicked in. Trading was halted. Smart investors watched their portfolios evaporate in real time. The news channels screamed "CRASH."

And then, something remarkable happened.

That night, TDP chief Chandrababu Naidu and JDU's Nitish Kumar pledged support to form the NDA government. The next morning, the Nifty opened with a gap-up of nearly 1,500 points. Over the following months, the index would go on to hit record highs 32 times in 2024, reaching approximately 26,000 by late September.

Think about that. Everyone who sold in panic on June 4 locked in catastrophic losses. Everyone who held—or had the courage to buy—recovered every single rupee and more within months.

Why do I start with this story? Because it perfectly illustrates the single most important truth about trading in Indian markets: **the market is not a rational machine. It is a crowd of human beings, and crowds are driven by emotion, not logic.**

The trader who understands this has a fighting chance. The trader who doesn't is simply transferring money from his pocket to someone else's.

---

## The Market as a Living Organism

Here is something that took me years to accept: the Indian stock market is not a math problem. You cannot solve it with formulas alone.

Every day, crores of buyers and sellers—retail investors, mutual funds, FIIs, insurance companies, proprietary desks—interact to determine prices. Each of these participants brings their own fears, greed, hopes, and ignorance to the table. The collective result is what we call "the market."

The market behaves less like a well-oiled machine and more like a herd of animals. When one buffalo starts running, the others follow—not because they know what the danger is, but because the simple act of running triggers a survival instinct. In the market, that instinct is called **herd behaviour**.

Consider what happened during the HDFC Bank crash in January 2024.

The stock fell 8.5% on January 16—its worst single-day decline since the Covid crash of 2020. The trigger was a quarterly results announcement that came in below expectations. Here is what is important: HDFC Bank is one of the most widely held stocks in India. Every mutual fund, every insurance company, every pension fund owned it. When it fell, the damage spread.

But here is the real lesson. The fall was not caused by the results alone. It was caused by the *reaction* to the results. Large institutional holders, worried about redemption pressure, sold preemptively. Retail holders, watching the stock fall, sold in panic. The selling fed on itself. By the end of the day, the Nifty and Sensex had recorded their worst session since June 2022—not because the entire economy had suddenly collapsed, but because one bank's quarterly profit was slightly lower than analysts expected.

This is how markets work. A single spark—a bad result, a political uncertainty, a global event—ignites a chain reaction of emotional decision-making. The initial move is often rational. The subsequent cascade is pure crowd psychology.

## The Two Masters: Fear and Greed

If the market is an emotional organism, its two primary emotions are fear and greed.

Greed is what makes you buy a stock that has already tripled because you are afraid of missing out. Fear is what makes you sell it at the bottom because you cannot bear to see your portfolio in red any longer.

Both emotions serve evolutionary purposes. Fear kept our ancestors from walking into predator dens. Greed motivated them to gather extra food for winter. But in the modern stock market—where decisions must be made in seconds, and where leverage multiplies every mistake—these ancient instincts become deadly liabilities.

Let me give you a concrete example.

In late 2023, the mid-cap and small-cap indices were rallying relentlessly. Fund managers were actually returning money to investors because they could not find enough quality stocks to deploy capital. Retail traders saw friends making 50%, 100%, 200% returns and felt an urgent, gnawing need to participate.

This is greed at work. It is the fear of missing out, dressed up as investment logic. "The trend is my friend," the trader tells himself. "This time is different." He buys at the top, often in illiquid small-caps where a few sells can wipe out months of gains.

Then the correction comes. Suddenly, the trader who was buying at 100 is watching the stock fall to 80, then 70, then 50. He tells himself he is a long-term investor. But when the stock hits 40, the pain becomes unbearable. He sells.

This is fear. It is the panic to escape a burning building, even if the fire is only in your imagination.

The stock—inevitably—recoverers. Not because the universe is conspiring against the trader, but because the stock was never worth 40. It was worth something closer to 100. The market had simply overshot in both directions, first from greed and then from fear.

## The Anchor Trap

There is another cognitive bias that destroys traders, especially in Indian markets. It is called **anchoring**.

Anchoring is the human tendency to fixate on a specific reference point—usually a recent high or low—and judge all subsequent prices relative to that point.

During the June 4 crash, thousands of traders anchored on the pre-election levels. They had seen the Nifty at 23,300. They watched it fall to 21,281. In their minds, the "real" value of the index was 23,300. Anything below that was a discount. They bought, hoping for a quick recovery to the anchor point.

But the recovery did not happen in a day. It took months. Many of these traders, leveraged and impatient, were shaken out well before the index returned to its anchor.

The correct way to think about it is this: the market has no memory. The fact that the Nifty was at 23,300 yesterday means nothing about where it will be tomorrow. Anchoring to a past price is like navigating by a star that has already burned out.

I have watched traders refuse to buy a stock at ₹500 because they "bought it at ₹800 earlier and lost money." The price has dropped 37%, but their anchor is still at 800. They are mentally stuck, unable to see the new opportunity because they are still reeling from the old wound.

Or worse, they watch a stock fall from ₹500 to ₹300 and start buying, convinced that it is a bargain because "it was at 500 before." But the stock might be falling for a very good reason—a promoter issue, a regulatory problem, a fundamental deterioration. The anchor of 500 is irrelevant.

## The Cost of Being Wrong

Let me now talk about something that beginners almost never consider: the cost structure of trading.

When you trade in Indian equity markets, you pay Securities Transaction Tax (STT) on both sides of every trade—buy and sell. You pay brokerage, exchange transaction charges, GST, stamp duty, and SEBI turnover fees. These costs add up.

Consider a day trader who turns over ₹1 crore in a month (not an unusual number for an active trader). The total transaction costs—including STT at 0.025% on sell side for delivery, or effectively 0.1% on both sides for intraday—could easily exceed ₹5,000 to ₹10,000 per month. This is money that leaves your account regardless of whether you made a profit or a loss.

Here is the killer statistic: more than 95% of participants in Indian equity derivatives lose money. Yet every single one of them—winners and losers alike—pays STT. This means that loss-makers are faithfully contributing to the government's tax kitty, even as their own capital erodes.

Think about that. No other business in India works this way. A restaurant owner pays income tax only if he makes a profit. But a trader pays transaction tax on every single trade, win or lose. The system is structurally tilted against the retail trader who trades frequently.

This is not a complaint. It is a fact you must internalize. If you are going to trade, you need to be profitable enough to overcome these costs. The market does not care about your expenses. You must earn your costs before you earn a single rupee of profit.

## The Feedback Loop of Failure

There is a pattern that repeats itself endlessly in the Indian markets. I call it the **feedback loop of failure**.

It works like this:

1. A new trader enters the market during a bull run. He buys a stock, it goes up, and he makes money. He attributes this to his intelligence.
2. Encouraged, he trades more frequently and with larger size. The market corrects. He loses money.
3. To recover his losses, he trades even more aggressively, often using options or leverage. He loses more.
4. Desperate, he abandons all discipline. He chases tips, buys at the top, sells at the bottom. Eventually, his account is substantially depleted.

The tragedy is that this trader never learns the real lesson. He blames the market, the brokers, the regulators, the politicians—everyone except himself. He was never defeated by a lack of strategy. He was defeated by his own psychology.

I have seen this pattern play out in every market cycle. During the Covid crash of March 2020, the same traders who sold in panic were the ones who bought at the top of the subsequent rally, only to be trapped again when the market corrected. The environment changes; the human response does not.

## The Institutional Edge

Let me address a question many beginners ask: "Do institutions trade better than retail?"

The answer is yes, but not for the reasons you think.

Institutions do not have better information—by the time a news item hits the terminal, it is common knowledge. They do not have better analysis—most mutual fund managers underperform the index. What they have is **better process**.

An institutional trader does not wake up in the morning and decide to buy or sell based on how he feels. He follows a checklist. He has predetermined risk limits. He has a team that reviews his decisions. He cannot double down on a losing position because he has compliance limits.

The retail trader has none of this. He is his own compliance officer, his own risk manager, his own fund manager. And human beings are terrible at regulating their own emotions.

Here is a simple test. Next time you take a trade, ask yourself: "Would I take this same trade if I were managing ₹100 crore of a client's money?" If the answer is no, you are probably making an emotional decision.

## Breaking the Cycle

How do you break the cycle of emotional trading?

First, you must accept that you are not special. Your brain is wired the same way as every other trader's. You will feel fear when the market drops 500 points. You will feel greed when you see a stock rallying 20% in a week. The goal is not to eliminate these feelings—that is impossible. The goal is to recognize them and act despite them.

Second, you need to build processes that overrule your emotions. This means:
- Pre-deciding your entry, target, and stop-loss before you enter a trade.
- Using limit orders instead of market orders during volatile sessions.
- Never adding to a losing position just to "average down."
- Taking profits systematically, not holding out for the exact top.

Third, you need to understand that **recovery math compounds**. During the June 4 crash, the Nifty fell 8.5% intraday. Many traders panicked because the fall was so sharp. But simple math shows that even a 6% decline can be recovered with modest monthly gains. As one experienced trader put it during that volatile period: "Even 1% per month closes a 5-6% gap within six months."

This is not optimism. It is arithmetic.

## The Round-Number Trap

There is one more psychological pattern worth mentioning: the tendency to obsess over round numbers.

Traders fixate on levels like 25,000 for the Nifty or 500 for a particular stock. They assume that these levels act as magical barriers, that the market "respects" them.

Here is the truth: round numbers have no inherent significance. They matter only because enough traders believe they matter, creating a self-fulfilling prophecy. But the first test of a round-number breakout usually fails. The smart approach is to wait for the close above to confirm the breakout before committing capital.

I watched this play out when the Nifty finally crossed 25,000 in September 2024. The index had made dozens of attempts, each one failing at the psychological level. Traders who bought the first breakout were trapped. Those who waited for the close above the level—and for the index to hold that level—were rewarded.

Patience is not passive. It is an active decision to let the market prove itself before you commit your capital.

---

## The Final Lesson

Let me leave you with this. The market is not your enemy. The market is not trying to take your money. The market is simply a mechanism that reflects the collective decisions of millions of participants.

The real enemy sits between your ears. It is the voice that tells you to buy when everyone is buying. It is the feeling that tells you to sell when the red candles keep coming. It is the ego that refuses to admit a mistake and the greed that refuses to take a profit.

If you can learn to recognize these voices without obeying them, you have already overcome the single biggest hurdle that causes most traders to lose.

Everything else—strategy, analysis, execution—is secondary.

---

### Key Takeaways

1. **The market is a crowd of emotional humans, not a rational machine.** Herd behaviour amplifies every move, creating buying frenzies and selling panics that have little to do with underlying value.

2. **Fear and greed are your two worst enemies.** They drive you to buy at tops and sell at bottoms. Recognize them when they arise, and act against your first instinct.

3. **Transaction costs (especially STT) are a structural drag.** More than 95% of derivatives traders lose money, yet all pay taxes on every trade. You must earn your costs before you earn profit.

4. **Anchoring to past prices destroys judgement.** The market has no memory. A stock that was at ₹800 yesterday is not "cheap" at ₹500 today unless your analysis confirms it.

5. **Build processes, not hopes.** Institutional traders win through process, not brilliance. Pre-decide your entry, stop-loss, and target. Do not change your plan based on how you feel in the moment.
