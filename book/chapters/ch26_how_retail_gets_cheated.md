# Chapter 26: The Predators – Inside Market Manipulation and Frauds

## The Day the Market Forgot to Breathe

On January 24, 2024, something unusual happened to India's most trusted bank stock. HDFC Bank, the ₹11 lakh crore behemoth that retail investors treat as a fixed deposit with growth potential, fell 8.5% in a single session. It was the worst single-day drop for the stock since the COVID crash of 2020. In six hours, investors lost over ₹1 lakh crore in market value.

The trigger? A quarterly earnings report that showed margins under pressure. Nothing fraudulent. Nothing illegal. Just a routine business disappointment amplified by the sheer weight of expectations.

But here's what caught my attention: the stock hit the lower circuit breaker. Trading halted. For forty-five minutes, no one could buy or sell HDFC Bank shares. The market's safety mechanism had kicked in—a circuit breaker designed to prevent panic selling from spiraling into a crash.

And yet, the damage was done. The Nifty fell 1.9% that day, dragged down by a single stock that accounts for nearly 12% of the index's weight. A concentrated position in one name had destabilized the entire market.

This wasn't a scam. But it was a warning.

The real predators don't need circuit breakers to stop them. They work in the shadows, exploiting the gaps between regulations, the blind spots in surveillance, and the trust of retail investors who believe the market is a level playing field.

It is not.

## The First Principles of Theft

Let me be clear about something from the start: the stock market is not a casino. It is a transfer mechanism. Money moves from one pocket to another. The question is whose pocket it ends up in.

In a fair market, capital flows to companies that create value. Investors who identify those companies early are rewarded. Those who buy late or sell early are penalized. This is the natural order of things—risk and reward, balanced by information and judgment.

But predators disrupt this order. They create artificial risk where none exists. They manufacture false information. They rig the game so that money flows to them regardless of whether the underlying companies succeed or fail.

Think of it like a poker game where one player can see everyone else's cards. He doesn't need to be a better player. He just needs to exploit the information asymmetry.

In the Indian stock market, this asymmetry has taken many forms over the decades. Some are crude. Some are sophisticated. All of them share one common feature: they rely on the retail investor being the last to know.

## The 1992 Scam: When Banks Became Betting Chips

I was not trading in 1992. I was in school, learning about supply and demand in economics class. But every market participant in India lives in the shadow of what happened that year.

Harshad Mehta was a broker who understood something that regulators did not: the banking system's settlement mechanism could be turned into a money-printing machine.

Here's how it worked in simple terms.

When one bank needed to borrow money from another bank, it issued a receipt called a Bank Receipt (BR). This was a short-term loan, usually for 15 days. The borrowing bank gave the BR to the lending bank as proof of the debt.

Mehta realized that these BRs could be traded. More importantly, he realized that banks did not always track their BRs carefully. So he began a practice called "accommodation"—getting banks to issue BRs without any actual lending taking place. These were fake receipts, backed by nothing.

With these fake BRs, Mehta could borrow money from the banking system. He used this money to buy stocks—specifically, a handful of stocks like ACC, Tata Steel, and Reliance. As he bought more, prices rose. As prices rose, his collateral was worth more, so he could borrow more money. It was a classic pyramid scheme, but executed through the banking system rather than through direct investor deposits.

At its peak, Mehta was buying stocks worth ₹1,000 crore in a single day. The market was drunk on liquidity. Prices doubled, tripled, quadrupled. Retail investors saw the frenzy and jumped in, buying at the top.

Then the scam unraveled. A journalist named Sucheta Dalal wrote an article in *The Times of India* exposing the BR fraud. The banks called in their loans. Mehta could not pay. The stocks crashed. Retail investors who had bought at inflated prices lost everything.

The total amount involved was estimated at ₹4,000 crore—a staggering sum in 1992, equivalent to roughly ₹40,000 crore today.

What did retail learn from this? Nothing lasting. The same pattern would repeat within a decade.

## The 2001 Crash: Circular Trading Goes Digital

Ketan Parekh was a different kind of predator. He understood that you don't need to manipulate the banking system if you can manipulate the stocks themselves.

Parekh focused on technology stocks—the darlings of the late 1990s boom. Companies like Global Trust Bank, Zee Telefilms, Himachal Futuristic Communications, and Pentamedia Graphics. These were stocks with high retail interest and limited floating supply.

His method was circular trading. Here's how it worked.

Parekh controlled a network of brokers and entities—some legitimate, some shell companies. He would buy shares of a target company through one broker, sell them to another entity he controlled, then buy them back through a third. The shares never really changed hands. They just moved in a circle.

But to the market, it looked like genuine volume. High volume attracted attention. Attention attracted retail buyers. As retail piled in, Parekh would start selling his actual holdings into the rising tide.

The numbers were enormous. In the case of Global Trust Bank, Parekh's group traded shares worth over ₹1,000 crore in a single year—far more than the bank's actual market capitalization. The stock price rose from around ₹100 to over ₹1,000.

When the music stopped in 2001, it was because the broader market was crashing globally. The tech bubble burst. Parekh's circular trading could not sustain the prices. His entities defaulted on payments to brokers. The dominoes fell.

The total market loss was estimated at ₹10,000 crore. Retail investors who had bought tech stocks at the peak saw their portfolios shrink by 80-90%.

## The Anatomy of a Pump-and-Dump

Let me walk you through a smaller, more common scam—the kind that happens every week in the Indian markets, often in small-cap and micro-cap stocks.

I call it the "three-act play."

**Act One: Accumulation**

The predator identifies a stock with low floating supply—perhaps a company with a small market capitalization, where the promoters hold 60-70% of the shares. The predator starts buying quietly through multiple accounts. Over weeks or months, he accumulates a significant position without moving the price much.

**Act Two: The Pump**

Now the predator needs attention. He spreads rumors through WhatsApp groups, Telegram channels, and paid newsletter services. The rumors are always the same: a big order is coming, a government contract is imminent, a foreign investor is buying, a buyback is planned.

Simultaneously, the predator starts trading among his own accounts to create artificial volume. The stock's daily turnover jumps from ₹10 lakh to ₹10 crore. The price chart shows a beautiful breakout.

Retail investors see the volume and the price action. They use technical analysis—breakout patterns, volume spikes, moving average crossovers—to justify buying. They don't know that the chart is manufactured.

**Act Three: The Dump**

Once the retail buying is strong enough, the predator starts selling. He sells into the rising tide, unloading his accumulated position at prices 200%, 300%, or 500% higher than his cost.

When the selling is done, the rumors stop. The volume dries up. The stock price collapses back to where it started—or lower.

Retail investors who bought at the top are left holding worthless shares. The predator walks away with crores.

Let me give you a worked example with numbers.

Suppose a stock trades at ₹50 with a daily volume of 10,000 shares. The predator buys 50,000 shares over two months at an average price of ₹55. His cost: ₹27.5 lakh.

He then starts the pump. He creates volume by trading among his own accounts—buying 5,000 shares from Account A, selling them to Account B, buying them back in Account C. The daily volume rises to 50,000 shares. The price moves to ₹150.

Retail sees the action. They buy 40,000 shares at an average price of ₹120. The predator sells his 50,000 shares at an average of ₹130. His proceeds: ₹65 lakh.

His profit: ₹65 lakh minus ₹27.5 lakh = ₹37.5 lakh.

The retail investors who bought at ₹120 now hold shares that will soon trade at ₹50 again. Their loss: ₹70 per share, or ₹28 lakh in total.

The predator made ₹37.5 lakh. The retail group lost ₹28 lakh. The remaining ₹9.5 lakh went to brokerage, taxes, and the cost of running the scam.

This is not a hypothetical. This pattern repeats hundreds of times every year in the Indian markets.

## Insider Trading: The Silent Theft

Pump-and-dump is loud. Insider trading is quiet. But it is far more pervasive.

Insider trading happens when someone with access to non-public information trades on that information before it becomes public. The company's CFO knows the quarterly results will be terrible. He sells his shares before the announcement. The stock drops 15% on the news. He saved himself from a loss. Someone else—a retail investor who bought that morning—took that loss instead.

The Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) has pursued insider trading cases aggressively in recent years. One notable case involved a senior executive at a large conglomerate who tipped off his relatives about an impending merger. The relatives bought shares before the announcement. When the merger was announced, the stock rose 20%. The relatives sold and made ₹2 crore in profit.

SEBI caught them through phone records and trading patterns. The executive was banned from the markets for five years. The relatives had to disgorge their profits plus interest.

But for every case that gets caught, dozens go undetected. Insider trading is inherently difficult to prove because you need to establish that the trader had access to specific non-public information and acted on it. Circumstantial evidence is not enough.

The real tragedy is that insider trading destroys the foundation of market trust. If retail investors believe that insiders always have an advantage, they stop participating. And a market without retail participation is a market without depth, without liquidity, without the democratic capital allocation that makes stock markets valuable.

## The Digital Frontier: Spoofing and HFT Manipulation

The newest predators don't use shell companies or circular trading. They use algorithms.

High-frequency trading (HFT) firms place thousands of orders per second. Most of these orders are never intended to be executed. They are placed to create a false impression of supply or demand.

This practice is called spoofing.

Here's how it works. A trader wants to sell 10,000 shares of a stock. But if he places a sell order for 10,000 shares, the price will drop. So instead, he places a large buy order—say, for 20,000 shares—at a price slightly above the current market. Other traders see this buy order and think demand is strong. They start buying. The price rises.

Once the price has risen enough, the trader cancels the fake buy order and sells his 10,000 shares at the higher price. The fake buy order was never meant to be filled. It was a mirage.

Spoofing is illegal in India. SEBI has fined several HFT firms for this practice. But detection is difficult because the orders exist for milliseconds before being cancelled.

The impact on retail investors is subtle but real. When you place a market order to buy 100 shares, you might get filled at a price that was artificially inflated by a spoof order that was cancelled before your order reached the exchange. You paid a few paise extra per share. Over thousands of trades, those paise add up to real money flowing from retail pockets to algorithmic predators.

## The Enablers: Brokers and Shell Companies

Predators rarely work alone. They need enablers.

Brokers are the first line of enablers. Some brokers turn a blind eye to suspicious trading patterns in their client accounts. Others actively participate, providing multiple trading accounts under different names to facilitate circular trading.

In the 2001 scam, several brokers were found to have allowed Ketan Parekh to use their client accounts for circular trading. They earned brokerage on the fake volume. When the scam collapsed, some brokers defaulted on their obligations to the exchange, causing a chain reaction that threatened the entire market.

Shell companies are the second line of enablers. These are companies with no real business operations—just a bank account, a registered address, and a board of directors who may not even know they are directors.

Predators use shell companies to hold shares, execute trades, and launder money. The shell company buys shares in the target stock. The predator pumps the stock. The shell company sells at a profit. The money is then transferred through a web of transactions to make it untraceable.

SEBI has cracked down on shell companies in recent years, identifying over 300 shell companies that were used for price manipulation. But new ones keep appearing. The cost of setting up a shell company in India is less than ₹50,000. The potential profit from a single pump-and-dump is crores. The economics favor the predator.

## The Timeline of Deception

Let me give you a timeline of major Indian market scams so you can see the pattern.

| Year | Scam | Method | Amount Involved | Retail Impact |
|------|------|--------|-----------------|---------------|
| 1992 | Harshad Mehta | Bank receipt fraud, stock price manipulation | ₹4,000 crore | Market crash, retail losses in ACC, Reliance, others |
| 2001 | Ketan Parekh | Circular trading in tech stocks | ₹10,000 crore | Tech stock collapse, retail portfolios halved |
| 2008 | Satyam fraud | Falsified financial statements | ₹7,000 crore | Stock fell from ₹544 to ₹6, retail wiped out |
| 2010-2015 | Various penny stock scams | Pump-and-dump through WhatsApp/Telegram | ₹500-1,000 crore each | Repeated losses in small-cap stocks |
| 2018 | IL&FS crisis | Corporate governance failure, hidden debt | ₹91,000 crore | Debt mutual fund losses, retail bondholders affected |
| 2021 | DHFL fraud | Diversion of funds, falsified loans | ₹34,000 crore | Bondholder losses, retail investors in debt schemes |

Each scam has a different method. But the pattern is always the same: a predator exploits a gap in the system, retail investors are the last to know, and they bear the losses.
