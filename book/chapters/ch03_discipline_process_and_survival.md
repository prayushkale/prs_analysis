# Chapter 3: Discipline, Process, and Survival

## The Trader's Mind

---

The Nifty had already fallen 400 points by 10:15 AM. By 11:30, it was down 800. The screens were bleeding red, and my phone would not stop buzzing. Messages from trading groups, frantic calls from friends who had bought positions the previous day, WhatsApp forwards claiming "this is just the beginning of a crash."

I sat in front of my three monitors, watching the carnage unfold. June 4, 2024. Election results day. The BJP-led NDA was falling short of expectations, and the market was pricing in uncertainty with brutal efficiency. Nifty would eventually touch 21,281—an intraday fall of nearly 8.5% from the previous close.

A trader I know—let me call him Rajeev—had been fully long since May. He had bought Nifty futures at 22,800, convinced the election verdict would be a landslide. By noon, his position was down ₹12 lakh. His margin account was flashing red. His broker called him three times asking for additional margin.

Rajeev called me at 12:15 PM. His voice was tight, controlled, but I could hear the panic underneath.

"Should I exit? I can't take this anymore. If it falls another 200 points, I'm wiped out."

I asked him one question: "What was your plan when you entered the trade?"

Silence.

"Plan? The plan was that Modi would win and markets would rally."

"That's not a plan," I said. "That's a hope dressed up as a trade."

## The Difference Between Hope and Process

Every trader who has survived more than five years in Indian markets will tell you the same thing: the market does not care about your hopes. It does not care about your analysis. It does not care about the money you need to make this month to pay your bills.

The market is a giant, emotionless machine that processes buy and sell orders. That is all it does.

The difference between a trader who survives and one who gets wiped out is not intelligence. It is not access to better data or faster internet. It is not even the ability to predict where the market will go next.

The difference is *process*.

A process is a set of rules you follow regardless of how you feel. It is the answer to every question the market throws at you, written down before the question is asked.

Rajeev had no process. He had a view. When the view was wrong, he had nothing to fall back on except hope and prayer. Hope is not a risk management strategy. Prayer is not a stop-loss.

## The Three Pillars of Trading Discipline

Over twenty years of trading Indian markets, I have broken almost every rule I am about to share with you. I have blown up accounts. I have taken losses that kept me awake for weeks. I have made decisions driven by fear, greed, and ego—and paid for every single one.

The discipline I teach now is not theoretical. It is carved from the scars of those mistakes.

There are three pillars that support every sustainable trading career:

### 1. Pre-Decision Rules

Before you enter a single trade, you must answer three questions:

**Where will I enter?** Not "around this level" or "if it looks good." A specific price or condition. "I will buy Nifty futures if it breaks 22,500 with volume." Not "I will buy if the market looks strong."

**Where will I exit if I am wrong?** This is your stop-loss. It must be a number, not a feeling. "I will exit if Nifty falls below 22,200." Not "I will exit if the trend reverses."

**Where will I exit if I am right?** Your target. Again, a specific number. "I will book 50% at 22,800 and trail the rest."

Write these three numbers on a piece of paper. Stick it to your monitor. Do not enter the trade until you have done this.

I learned this lesson the hard way in 2008. I bought Reliance at ₹2,600 because a friend's "tip" said it would go to ₹3,000. I had no stop-loss. When it fell to ₹2,400, I told myself it was a buying opportunity. At ₹2,200, I stopped answering my broker's calls. At ₹1,800, I had lost more than I could afford.

The stock eventually recovered. I did not. I had sold at the bottom, driven by panic, because I had no plan for what to do when the trade went against me.

### 2. Position Sizing

This is the single most important concept in trading, and the one most retail traders ignore.

The question is not "How much can I make?" The question is "How much can I lose without it affecting my ability to trade tomorrow?"

Here is a simple rule: **Never risk more than 1-2% of your trading capital on any single trade.**

Let me show you the math.

Suppose you have a trading account of ₹5 lakh. You identify a trade in Tata Motors. You plan to buy at ₹1,000 and set a stop-loss at ₹950. That is a risk of ₹50 per share.

With 1% risk, you can afford to lose ₹5,000 on this trade. So your position size is:

₹5,000 ÷ ₹50 = 100 shares

That is one lot of Tata Motors futures, or 100 shares in the cash market.

If the trade hits your stop-loss, you lose ₹5,000. Your account goes to ₹4,95,000. You are still in the game. You can take another trade tomorrow.

Now consider the alternative. You are convinced this trade is a winner. You put your entire ₹5 lakh into buying 500 shares. The stock falls to ₹950. You have lost ₹25,000—5% of your capital. Do this four times in a row, and your account is down 20%. Do it ten times, and you are out.

The market does not care that you were "sure." The market does not care that your analysis was perfect. The market only cares about what happens next, and no one knows what happens next.

Position sizing is the only tool you have to ensure that you survive long enough to be right.

### 3. Emotional Detachment

This is the hardest pillar to build, because it goes against every human instinct.

When you have money in a trade, your brain treats it differently. The amygdala—the part of your brain that processes fear—lights up. Your heart rate increases. Your palms sweat. You start looking for reasons to hold a losing position and reasons to exit a winning one too early.

This is biology. You cannot eliminate it. But you can build systems that bypass it.

The most effective system I know is the **mechanical stop-loss**.

In Indian markets, you can place a stop-loss order that is held by the exchange. If the price hits your level, the order executes automatically. You do not have to think. You do not have to decide. You do not have to watch the screen and feel the panic rising.

I have seen traders sit in front of a falling stock, telling themselves "it will bounce," watching their profits turn into losses and their losses turn into disasters. Every single one of them had the ability to place a stop-loss. They chose not to.

Why? Because placing a stop-loss feels like admitting you might be wrong. And our egos hate that.

But here is the truth: **every trader is wrong about half the time.** The best traders in the world have win rates of 40-50%. The difference is that when they are wrong, they lose small. When they are right, they let their profits run.

A mechanical stop-loss is the tool that makes this possible.

## The Worked Example: A Real Trade

Let me walk you through a trade I actually took in early 2024. I am sharing this not to impress you, but to show you how process works in practice.

**The Setup:**

In January 2024, HDFC Bank reported Q3 results that disappointed the market. The stock fell 8.5% in a single day—its worst drop since the COVID crash in March 2020. The Nifty fell with it, losing over 1,600 points in two days.

The news was bad. The stock was getting hammered. Every analyst on television was saying "avoid private banks" and "the worst is not over."

But I had been watching HDFC Bank for months. I knew that the stock had strong support around ₹1,400-1,420, a level it had held multiple times over the previous year. The fall had taken it to ₹1,460. One more bad day, and it would hit that support.

**The Process:**

I did not buy immediately. I waited.

On January 18, the stock opened at ₹1,430 and fell to ₹1,410 before bouncing. The support held. Volume was heavy—institutions were buying the dip.

That evening, I wrote down my plan:

- **Entry:** Buy 200 shares (2 lots in futures) at ₹1,430 if the stock opens flat or higher the next day
- **Stop-loss:** ₹1,380 (₹50 per share risk = ₹10,000 total risk, which was 1.2% of my capital)
- **Target 1:** ₹1,550 (book 100 shares, profit of ₹12,000)
- **Target 2:** ₹1,650 (trail stop on remaining 100 shares to ₹1,500)

**The Execution:**

The next day, HDFC Bank opened at ₹1,435. I bought 200 shares at ₹1,432.

Over the next week, the stock moved sideways. It touched ₹1,390 twice but never hit my stop-loss. I did nothing. I did not adjust my stop-loss. I did not add to my position. I followed my plan.

On January 29, the stock broke out. It hit ₹1,550 on February 2. I sold 100 shares as planned, booking ₹11,800 profit.

I moved my stop-loss on the remaining 100 shares to ₹1,500.

The stock continued to rally. By February 15, it was at ₹1,620. My trailing stop was now at ₹1,570. On February 20, the stock fell back to ₹1,570 and my stop-loss triggered. I sold the remaining 100 shares at ₹1,570, booking an additional ₹13,800 profit.

**The Result:**

Total profit: ₹25,600 on a risk of ₹10,000. A risk-reward ratio of 2.56:1.

More importantly, I never once felt anxious during this trade. I knew exactly what would happen at every price level. When the stock fell to ₹1,390, I did not panic. When it broke out, I did not get greedy. I followed my process.

## The Survival Mindset

Trading is not about being right. It is about surviving long enough to let your edge play out.

Think of it like this: if you flip a coin that is weighted 55% in your favour, you will make money over 1,000 flips. But you will also have losing streaks of 5, 10, even 15 flips in a row. If you bet your entire bankroll on each flip, you will go bankrupt during one of those losing streaks—even though the odds are in your favour.

The same is true in trading. Your edge might be real. Your analysis might be excellent. But if you risk too much on any single trade, a random string of losses will wipe you out.

This is why survival is the first priority. Not profits. Not being right. Survival.

I have seen brilliant traders—people who could read charts better than anyone I know—leave the markets forever because they could not control their emotions. I have seen mediocre traders—people with average analysis and average instincts—build substantial wealth because they had discipline.

The market rewards consistency, not brilliance.

## The Enemy Within

Let me be honest with you about something I have struggled with for years: the desire to trade.

When I first started, I traded every day. I felt like I was missing out if I was not in a position. I would scan charts for hours, looking for any excuse to enter a trade. I would take setups that were barely there, convincing myself that "this time is different."

This is called **overtrading**, and it is a form of addiction.

The dopamine hit of a winning trade is real. Your brain releases pleasure chemicals when you make money. And like any addiction, you need bigger and bigger doses to get the same feeling.

The cure is simple but brutal: **trade less.**

I now take, on average, 2-4 trades per month. Some months, I take zero. I wait for setups that meet my criteria perfectly. If they do not appear, I do nothing.

Doing nothing is the hardest skill in trading. But it is also the most profitable.

## The Extended Hours Trap

In late 2024, Indian exchanges introduced extended trading hours. The market would now open at 9:00 AM and close at 3:30 PM, with a pre-open session starting at 8:30 AM.

This was sold as a benefit for traders. "More time to react to global news." "Better alignment with international markets."

But for the undisciplined trader, extended hours are a trap.

More hours mean more opportunities to make impulsive decisions. More time to watch the screen and feel the emotional pull of every tick. More chances to overtrade.

I have a simple rule: **I do not trade in the first 30 minutes or the last 30 minutes of the session.**

The first 30 minutes are driven by overnight news and institutional orders. The last 30 minutes are driven by position squaring and FII flows. Neither period is suitable for retail traders with small capital.

Between 9:30 AM and 3:00 PM, the market is driven by genuine supply and demand. That is where I operate.

## The Final Lesson

Rajeev, the trader I mentioned at the beginning of this chapter, did not exit his position on June 4. He held, hoping for a recovery. By 2:30 PM, his loss had grown to ₹18 lakh. His broker liquidated his position at 2:45 PM, selling his Nifty futures at 21,350.

The next day, the Nifty opened at 22,100. By the end of the week, it was at 23,200. By September, it had hit 26,000—a new all-time high.

Rajeev had been right about the direction. He just could not survive the volatility to get there.

He stopped trading after that. He told me he could not take the stress anymore. I do not blame him. He had lost not just money, but the confidence that he could control his own decisions.

That is the real cost of poor discipline. It is not the money you lose. It is the belief that you can ever be a successful trader.

Build your process. Write down your rules. Follow them without exception.

The market will test you. It will tempt you. It will try to break you.

But if you have discipline, you will survive. And if you survive long enough, you will win.

---

## Key Takeaways

1. **Write down your entry, stop-loss, and target before entering any trade.** Do not enter a trade until these three numbers are on paper. This is non-negotiable.

2. **Risk no more than 1-2% of your capital on any single trade.** Calculate your position size based on the distance to your stop-loss, not on how confident you feel.

3. **Use mechanical stop-losses held by the exchange.** Remove the emotional decision of exiting a losing trade. Let the system do the work.

4. **Trade less, not more.** The best trades are the ones you do not take. Wait for setups that meet your criteria perfectly. Overtrading is a form of addiction.

5. **Survival is the first priority.** You cannot make money if you are out of the game. Every decision should be made with the question: "Will this help me trade tomorrow?"
