# Chapter 1: Why Most Traders Lose

> *"Stock market is where people with money meet people with experience — then the people with experience leave with the money, and the people with money leave with the experience."*

Every year, hundreds of thousands of people open their first trading account. Many come believing that stock markets are a shortcut to wealth — a place where a small account and a few hours a day can replace a lifetime of salary. The data tells a different story.

In 2024, India's market regulator SEBI published a study that made headlines across the country: **93% of individual F&O traders lost money** over the three-year period studied. Only 6% made a profit below ₹1 lakh per year, and just **1% of retail traders** earned more than ₹1 lakh annually from their trading activity. The bottom 3.5% lost an average of ₹28 lakh per year — enough to destabilise a middle-class household permanently.

This chapter asks why. The answer is not a single mistake but a chain of them — structural, psychological, and informational. Each one alone is survivable. Together, they guarantee that most entrants lose everything before they learn anything.


## 1. The Gambler's Frame

Most people come to derivatives not as business owners but as gamblers. They see a low premium on an option — ₹100, ₹200 — and treat it like a lottery ticket. The smaller the payment, the less real the risk feels. This is the first and most dangerous structural error.

An option buyer pays a premium. If the market does not move far enough and fast enough, that premium expires worthless. **The buyer needs the market to move in the right direction, by a specific amount, within a specific time frame, and the move must be large enough to overcome theta decay and implied-volatility contraction.** The probability of all four conditions aligning is low.

One of the early transcripts in this corpus frames it sharply:

> *"Every day I switch on TV, I find some people die of accidents. Are we going to stop traveling? The probability of winning for an option buyer is less than 20%."*

Twenty percent probability of profit means an eighty percent probability of loss. A buyer who repeats this bet enough times will, by simple arithmetic, run out of capital. This is not a matter of bad luck — it is the mathematics of the instrument.

The seller, by contrast, collects premium. The market can go up, down, or sideways — any scenario except a violent, sustained directional move generates a profit for the seller. The seller's edge is not prediction but positioning. For every option that is bought, one is sold. If buying were strictly better, no seller would exist.

> *"Option selling does not need extraordinary skill. You just need to have extraordinary capital."*

A buyer with ₹2,500 can buy one lot of a near-the-money option. A seller with the same ₹2,500 cannot sell anything — the margin requirement for a naked short option exceeds the buyer's entire capital by an order of magnitude. The seller posts collateral in the form of bank fixed deposits, shares, or mutual funds, and earns interest on that collateral even before the trade itself makes a rupee. **The buyer assumes risk with cash; the seller deploys capital that earns elsewhere regardless.**

This asymmetry — the buyer's apparent convenience versus the seller's structural advantage — is invisible to the new trader who sees only a cheap premium and dreams of a 10-bagger.


## 2. The Three Myths of Option Buying

New option buyers are drawn by three claims: *limited risk, unlimited profit potential, and low upfront cost.* Each is true in theory and misleading in practice.

**Myth one: Limited risk.** The buyer's maximum loss is indeed the premium paid. But if you lose the entire premium on five consecutive trades, you are out of the game. The "limited loss" is not a risk-management tool — it is a natural expiry of the ticket. A seller, by contrast, can adjust a losing position by hedging — shorting futures, selling additional options, or rolling forward. A buyer has no equivalent escape. Once the market moves against the position, the buyer can only watch the premium decay.

**Myth two: Unlimited profit potential.** A long call's profit is theoretically unlimited. But Nifty has rarely moved more than 10-12% in any given month. The practical range is a few hundred points. The "unlimited" framing creates an expectation mismatch: the buyer dreams of a ten-bagger while the market delivers at best a 2:1 or 3:1 return on a good trade. On most trades, theta decay consumes the premium before the expected move materialises.

**Myth three: Low cost.** The premium is low because the probability of profit is low. Buying a far-OTM option for ₹15 is not cheap — it is evidence that the market assigns a very small chance to that strike being reached before expiry. Paying ₹15 for a 5% chance of winning is more expensive, in expected-value terms, than paying ₹500 for a 50% chance. The buyer chases leverage and calls it affordability.

> *"With limited capital, if you want unlimited profit, either the risk is very high or the probability of winning is very low."*


## 3. The Journey to Failure

The typical trajectory of a losing trader follows a pattern so consistent that it was documented as a dedicated series in the source material, titled *Why NO To Trading.*

**Stage one: Novice luck.** The new trader buys a call option on a trending market. The market moves in their favour, and the trade doubles in a week. The trader attributes this to skill and doubles down — larger quantity, shorter expiry, more aggressive strikes.

**Stage two: The revenge trade.** One trade goes wrong. The trader, instead of pausing, immediately places a second trade to "recover" the loss. This second trade is usually larger, shorter-dated, and more speculative. It loses too. The account is now down 20-30%.

**Stage three: The search for certainty.** The trader begins hunting for the perfect system. They buy courses, join Telegram channels, follow social-media tips, switch from buying to selling and back again. The underlying belief is that there is a secret — a method the professionals know — that will fix everything. This is a misunderstanding of how markets work. There is no safe strategy because there is no static edge.

> *"There is no such thing called a safe strategy. There is no permanently fundamentally strong company. Reliance Communications was once the most valuable telecom company in India. It went to zero."*

**Stage four: Leverage acceleration.** To recover past losses, the trader increases position size. A small account is now using the maximum permitted leverage on far-OTM weekly options. One adverse day — a 200-point gap against the position — wipes out the account entirely. The lender of last resort has to sell the house. The trader exits the market permanently, convinced it is rigged.

This journey is not a failure of character. It is a failure of education and capital structure. The trader was never taught that the market does not reward prediction — it rewards positioning, patience, and the ability to survive long enough for volatility to work in your favour.


## 4. The Information Asymmetry

Retail traders believe they are trading against the market. In truth, they are trading against the most sophisticated institutional infrastructure ever built — algorithmic desks with co-located servers, HNI groups with direct market access, and foreign portfolio investors who move billions of dollars in minutes.

When RBI wrote off the equity of Lakshmi Vilas Bank in November 2020, the share continued to trade on the exchange for days. Retail buyers who saw a ₹20 stock and thought they were getting a bargain discovered that regulatory write-offs can make equity valueless regardless of the traded price. The exchange did not halt trading. The information was public but not evenly absorbed.

> *"Stock market is where people with money meet people with experience — then the people with experience leave with the money, and the people with money leave with the experience."*

This asymmetry is structural, not conspiratorial. Institutions have dedicated research teams, direct access to company management, and the ability to absorb large blocks at negotiated prices. The retail trader's advantage is speed in small sizes — the ability to enter and exit without moving the price. But this advantage only works when the trader has a framework, not when they are reacting to tips and headlines.


## 5. Skill Before Size

The COVID-19 lockdown created a generation of new retail traders in India. Zerodha's new accounts tripled. CDSL account openings surged by millions. A cohort of first-time traders learned technical analysis from YouTube tutorials and began trading during the most volatile market environment in a decade.

Many made money — the market recovered faster than any professional expected, and a simple buy-and-hold strategy on blue-chip stocks produced remarkable returns. But as Dhanraj noted at the time:

> *"The market can stay below its peak for thirty years — make sure you acquire skill before you risk size."*

The Nikkei index reached 40,000 in 1989. It did not reclaim that level for over thirty years. The Shanghai Composite hit 6,000 in 2007 and, at the time of writing, had not returned to it. "It always comes back" is true over very long horizons, but a trader's capitulation date is often much closer than the recovery.

The right question is not "how much can I make?" but **"how much can I lose without changing my life?"** A trader who can survive a 50% drawdown and continue trading with discipline has a structural advantage over a trader who is wiped out by a single gap opening. Survival is not a virtue — it is the prerequisite for edge to compound.


## 6. The Real Edge

The most consistent lesson across the thousands of market days documented in this book is that **consensus is dangerous.** When every social-media post is bearish, the short trade is already crowded. When a stock is on every broker's "buy" list, the institutional sellers are already distributing their holdings.

> *"Good news is good news. Very good news is bad news. Bad news is bad news. Very bad news is good news."*

This is not contrarianism for its own sake. It is a structural observation: markets price the future, not the present. A very bad quarter — like the pandemic's first wave — invites government and central bank intervention. The policy response, not the news itself, drives the subsequent rally. By the time the news feels survivable, the market has already repriced.

The trader who understands this stops trying to predict the next budget number and starts positioning for how the market will *react* to the number. That is a fundamentally different skill — and the one that separates the 1% from the 93%.

---

### Key Takeaways

1. The SEBI data is definitive: 93% of retail F&O traders lose money. This is structural, not a skill deficit that can be overcome by more effort.

2. Option buying has an inherent probability disadvantage: the buyer wins only in one of five market scenarios. Selling is statistically superior but requires capital.

3. The typical losing trajectory — novice luck, revenge trade, system-chasing, leverage acceleration — is predictable and avoidable.

4. Information asymmetry between retail and institutional participants is permanent. The retail edge is speed in small sizes, not prediction.

5. Survival is the only prerequisite for long-term edge. Size must follow skill, not precede it.

6. Consensus views are already priced in. The trader's job is to anticipate the market's reaction, not the event itself.
